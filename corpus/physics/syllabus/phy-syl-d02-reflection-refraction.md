## What the syllabus requires

State the laws of reflection and describe images formed by plane and curved
mirrors, using the mirror formula. State the laws of refraction and use Snell's
law, the refractive index as a ratio of speeds, and the real and apparent depth
relation. Define the critical angle, explain total internal reflection and give
its applications.

## Reflection at plane and curved mirrors

The laws of reflection are that the incident ray, the reflected ray and the normal
at the point of incidence all lie in the same plane, and that the angle of
incidence equals the angle of reflection.

The image in a plane mirror is virtual, erect, the same size as the object,
laterally inverted, and as far behind the mirror as the object is in front. Two
plane mirrors inclined at an angle theta form 360 divided by theta, minus one,
images of a point object. Two mirrors at 60 degrees give 360/60 - 1 = 5 images.

For a curved (spherical) mirror the focal length is half the radius of curvature,
f = r/2, and the mirror formula is

1/f = 1/u + 1/v,

with the magnification m = v/u = image height divided by object height. Using the
real-is-positive convention, a real object, a real image and a concave mirror all
take positive values, while a virtual image and a convex mirror take negative
values.

A concave mirror of focal length 10 cm with an object 15 cm away gives
1/v = 1/10 - 1/15 = (3 - 2)/30 = 1/30, so v = 30 cm and m = 30/15 = 2. The image
is real, inverted and twice as large. A convex mirror always gives a virtual,
erect, diminished image, which is why it is used as a driving mirror and in shops.

## Refraction and Snell's law

Refraction is the change in direction of a ray of light as it passes from one
medium into another of different optical density, caused by the change in the
speed of light. A ray passing into a denser medium bends towards the normal; a ray
passing into a less dense medium bends away from the normal. A ray along the
normal is not bent.

Snell's law states that the ratio of the sine of the angle of incidence to the
sine of the angle of refraction is a constant for a given pair of media:

n = sin i divided by sin r.

The refractive index can also be written as the speed of light in vacuum divided
by the speed of light in the medium, so glass of refractive index 1.5 slows light
to 3 times 10 to the power 8 divided by 1.5, that is 2 times 10 to the power 8 m/s.

For an object seen through a flat surface,

n = real depth divided by apparent depth.

A coin at a real depth of 12 cm in a liquid of refractive index 1.5 appears to be
at 12/1.5 = 8 cm, so it seems to be raised by 4 cm. This is why a swimming pool
looks shallower than it is and why a stick in water looks bent.

## The critical angle and total internal reflection

When light travels from a denser to a less dense medium and the angle of incidence
is increased, the refracted ray bends further from the normal until at one
particular angle of incidence the refracted ray grazes the surface at 90 degrees.
That angle of incidence is the critical angle C, and

sin C = 1 divided by n.

Beyond the critical angle no light is refracted at all: it is all reflected back
into the denser medium, which is total internal reflection. The two conditions are
that the light must be passing from a denser to a less dense medium, and the angle
of incidence must be greater than the critical angle.

For glass of refractive index 1.5, sin C = 0.667 and C = 41.8 degrees. For water
of refractive index 4/3, sin C = 0.75 and C = 48.6 degrees.

Total internal reflection explains the mirage, the sparkle of a cut diamond, and
the working of optical fibres, prism periscopes and prism binoculars.
