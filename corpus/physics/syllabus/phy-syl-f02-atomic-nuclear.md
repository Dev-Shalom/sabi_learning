## What the syllabus requires

Describe the Thomson, Rutherford and Bohr models of the atom. Use the notation for
atomic number, mass number and isotopes. State the photoelectric equation and use
it, and explain the wave-particle duality of matter. Describe the properties of
alpha, beta and gamma radiation, balance nuclear equations, and use the idea of
half-life. Distinguish nuclear fission from fusion and state the mass-energy
relation.

## Models of the atom

Thomson pictured the atom as a sphere of positive charge with electrons embedded
in it, like seeds in a fruit.

Rutherford's alpha-scattering experiment replaced that picture. Most alpha
particles fired at a thin gold foil passed straight through, showing that the atom
is mostly empty space; a few were deflected through large angles and a very few
came almost straight back, showing that the positive charge and nearly all the
mass are concentrated in a very small, dense nucleus.

Bohr added that electrons move in fixed orbits, or energy levels, without
radiating, and that a photon is emitted or absorbed only when an electron jumps
between levels. The photon energy is the difference between the two levels, and
this explains why the spectrum of an element is a set of sharp lines rather than a
continuous band.

## Notation, energy levels and the photoelectric effect

A nuclide is written with its mass number A above and its atomic number Z below
the symbol. The atomic number Z is the number of protons, the mass number A is the
number of protons plus neutrons, and the number of neutrons is A minus Z. Isotopes
are atoms of the same element with the same Z but different A, such as
carbon-12 and carbon-14; they are chemically identical because chemistry depends
on Z.

The energy of a photon is E = h f, where h is the Planck constant,
6.6 times 10 to the power -34 J s. Since c = f lambda, the energy can also be
written h c divided by lambda.

The photoelectric effect is the emission of electrons from a metal surface when
light of high enough frequency falls on it. Einstein's photoelectric equation is

h f = W + one half m v squared (maximum),

where W is the work function, the minimum energy needed to free an electron. The
threshold frequency is f0 = W divided by h, and below it no electrons are emitted
however bright the light, which is the observation that the wave picture of light
could not explain. Increasing the intensity increases the number of electrons but
not their maximum kinetic energy; increasing the frequency increases their maximum
kinetic energy.

Light of frequency 1.0 times 10 to the power 15 Hz has photon energy
6.6 times 10 to the power -34 times 10 to the power 15, that is
6.6 times 10 to the power -19 J. Falling on a metal of work function
3.0 times 10 to the power -19 J, it gives the emitted electrons a maximum kinetic
energy of 3.6 times 10 to the power -19 J, and the threshold frequency of that
metal is 3.0 times 10 to the power -19 divided by 6.6 times 10 to the power -34,
about 4.5 times 10 to the power 14 Hz.

Matter shows the same duality. A moving particle has an associated wavelength
lambda = h divided by momentum, the de Broglie relation, and electron diffraction
is the experimental evidence for it.

## Radioactivity and half-life

Radioactivity is the spontaneous disintegration of an unstable nucleus with the
emission of radiation. It is unaffected by temperature, pressure or chemical
combination.

An alpha particle is a helium nucleus, with charge +2 and mass number 4. It is the
most strongly ionising and the least penetrating, stopped by a sheet of paper or a
few centimetres of air, and it is deflected by electric and magnetic fields.

A beta particle is a fast electron, with charge -1 and negligible mass. It is
moderately ionising, stopped by a few millimetres of aluminium, and it is deflected
more strongly than an alpha particle and in the opposite direction.

A gamma ray is a high-frequency electromagnetic wave with no charge and no mass.
It is the least ionising and the most penetrating, needing several centimetres of
lead, and it is not deflected by electric or magnetic fields.

In a nuclear equation the mass numbers must balance and the atomic numbers must
balance. Alpha emission lowers A by 4 and Z by 2, so uranium-238 decaying by alpha
emission gives thorium-234 with Z falling from 92 to 90. Beta emission leaves A
unchanged and raises Z by 1, because a neutron becomes a proton and an electron.
Gamma emission changes neither A nor Z.

The half-life is the time for half the nuclei in a sample to decay, or for the
activity to fall to half its value. After n half-lives the amount remaining is the
original amount multiplied by one half to the power n. A 40 g sample with a
half-life of 5 days has 5 g left after 15 days, which is 3 half-lives. The decay
constant is related to the half-life by lambda = 0.693 divided by the half-life.

## Fission, fusion and mass-energy

Nuclear fission is the splitting of a heavy nucleus, such as uranium-235, into two
lighter nuclei with the release of neutrons and a large amount of energy. The
released neutrons can cause further fissions, giving a chain reaction, which is
controlled in a reactor by boron or cadmium control rods that absorb neutrons and
by a moderator such as graphite or heavy water that slows them down.

Nuclear fusion is the joining of two light nuclei into a heavier one with the
release of energy; it needs extremely high temperature and pressure and is the
source of the energy of the Sun and the stars.

In both processes the total mass of the products is slightly less than the total
mass of the reactants, and the missing mass, the mass defect, appears as energy
according to E = m c squared. The energy equivalent of that mass defect is the
binding energy of the nucleus.
