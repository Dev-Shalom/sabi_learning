## What the syllabus requires

Distinguish conductors, semiconductors and insulators. Explain intrinsic and
extrinsic semiconductors and the effect of doping. Describe the p-n junction diode
under forward and reverse bias and its use in half-wave and full-wave
rectification. Describe the transistor and its use as an amplifier and as a
switch. Describe thermionic emission and the cathode-ray oscilloscope.

## Conductors, semiconductors and insulators

A conductor has many free electrons and a very low resistivity: copper, aluminium,
silver. An insulator has almost no free charge carriers and a very high
resistivity: rubber, glass, dry wood, plastic. A semiconductor lies between the
two: silicon and germanium are the important examples.

The resistance of a metal rises with temperature, because the lattice ions vibrate
more and scatter the electrons. The resistance of a semiconductor falls with
temperature, because heating frees more charge carriers. That difference is the
simplest experimental test of which kind of material you have, and it is why a
thermistor is used as a temperature sensor.

A pure semiconductor is called intrinsic. In it the number of free electrons
equals the number of holes, a hole being the vacancy left where an electron is
missing, which behaves as a mobile positive charge.

## Doping and the p-n junction

Doping is the deliberate addition of a tiny amount of impurity to a pure
semiconductor to increase its conductivity, giving an extrinsic semiconductor.

Adding a pentavalent impurity such as phosphorus, arsenic or antimony supplies a
spare electron for each impurity atom, giving an n-type semiconductor in which
electrons are the majority carriers. Adding a trivalent impurity such as boron,
gallium, indium or aluminium leaves a hole for each impurity atom, giving a p-type
semiconductor in which holes are the majority carriers. Both types are electrically
neutral overall.

A p-n junction is formed where a p-type region meets an n-type region. Electrons
and holes diffuse across and recombine near the boundary, leaving a thin depletion
layer with no mobile carriers and a potential barrier across it.

In forward bias the positive terminal of the supply is joined to the p side. The
barrier is reduced, the depletion layer narrows, and a large current flows. In
reverse bias the positive terminal is joined to the n side. The barrier is
increased, the depletion layer widens, and only a very small leakage current
flows. A junction diode therefore conducts in one direction only, which is what
makes it a rectifier.

## Rectification and the transistor

Rectification is the conversion of alternating current to direct current. A single
diode in series with the load gives half-wave rectification: only one half of each
cycle is passed and the other half is blocked, so the output is in pulses with
gaps. Four diodes in a bridge arrangement, or two diodes with a centre-tapped
transformer, give full-wave rectification: both halves of the cycle drive current
through the load in the same direction. A capacitor connected across the load
smooths the output by charging at the peaks and discharging into the load between
them.

A transistor has three regions and three terminals: emitter, base and collector.
It is made either as n-p-n or as p-n-p, with a very thin, lightly doped base
between a heavily doped emitter and a collector. The three currents are related by

emitter current = base current + collector current.

A base current of 0.02 A with a collector current of 1.98 A gives an emitter
current of 2.00 A, and the current gain, collector current divided by base
current, is 1.98/0.02 = 99. A small change in the base current therefore produces
a large change in the collector current, which is how the transistor amplifies.

Used as a switch, the transistor is driven either into saturation, where it
conducts fully and behaves like a closed switch, or into cut-off, where almost no
collector current flows and it behaves like an open switch. With a light-dependent
resistor or a thermistor in the base circuit this gives an automatic light or
temperature switch, with no moving parts.

## Thermionic emission and the cathode-ray oscilloscope

Thermionic emission is the release of electrons from the surface of a heated metal.
The electrons gain enough energy from the heating to escape the attraction of the
metal, and the number emitted rises with the temperature of the cathode and with
the nature of its surface.

A cathode-ray tube uses thermionic emission to produce an electron beam, which is
accelerated by an anode at a high positive potential, deflected by the horizontal
and vertical deflecting plates, and made visible where it strikes a fluorescent
screen. The cathode-ray oscilloscope built from it is used to display waveforms,
to measure voltages and small time intervals, and to measure frequency.
