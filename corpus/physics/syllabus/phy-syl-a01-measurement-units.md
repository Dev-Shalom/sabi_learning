## What the syllabus requires

Name the fundamental quantities and their SI units, and derive the units of other
quantities from them. Use the common prefixes and standard form. Read a metre
rule, a vernier calliper and a micrometer screw gauge, and state the precision of
each. Recognise the main sources of experimental error and say how to reduce
them. Use dimensions to test whether an equation is possible.

## Fundamental and derived units

There are seven fundamental quantities. Length is measured in metres (m), mass in
kilograms (kg), time in seconds (s), electric current in amperes (A),
thermodynamic temperature in kelvin (K), amount of substance in moles (mol), and
luminous intensity in candelas (cd).

Every other unit is built from these. Force is mass times acceleration, so the
newton is 1 kg m/s squared. Work is force times distance, so the joule is 1 N m,
that is 1 kg m squared per second squared. Power is work per second, so the watt
is 1 J/s. Pressure is force per unit area, so the pascal is 1 N/m squared.

Prefixes shorten the powers of ten: milli is 10 to the power -3, micro is 10 to
the power -6, nano is 10 to the power -9, kilo is 10 to the power 3, mega is 10
to the power 6, giga is 10 to the power 9. So 2.5 kN is 2500 N, and 40 microfarad
is 4.0 times 10 to the power -5 F.

## Reading the measuring instruments

A metre rule reads to the nearest millimetre, that is 0.1 cm. Read it with the eye
directly above the mark, otherwise parallax shifts the reading.

A vernier calliper reads to 0.01 cm. Take the main-scale reading just before the
vernier zero, then find the vernier division that lines up exactly with a
main-scale division and multiply it by 0.01 cm. If the main scale reads 2.4 cm and
the sixth vernier division coincides, the length is 2.4 + 0.06 = 2.46 cm.

A micrometer screw gauge reads to 0.01 mm. Add the thimble reading to the sleeve
reading. If the sleeve shows 5.5 mm and the thimble shows 28 divisions, the
reading is 5.5 + 0.28 = 5.78 mm. Check for a zero error first and subtract it,
with its sign, from every reading.

## Errors and dimensions

Accuracy is how close a reading is to the true value; precision is how finely the
instrument divides the scale. A systematic error, such as a zero error or a badly
calibrated scale, shifts every reading the same way and does not average out. A
random error, such as a reaction-time error in timing, does average out if the
measurement is repeated and the mean taken.

The dimensions of a quantity are its expression in mass M, length L and time T.
Velocity is L per T, so its dimension is L T to the power -1. Acceleration is
L T to the power -2. Force is M L T to the power -2. An equation can only be
correct if both sides have the same dimensions. Test v squared = u squared + 2as:
the left side is L squared T to the power -2, and 2as is L T to the power -2 times
L, which is also L squared T to the power -2. The equation passes the test.

A dimensional check can show that an equation is wrong, but it cannot show that it
is right, because a dimensionless constant such as one half or 2 pi leaves the
dimensions unchanged.

## Not on this syllabus

Propagating uncertainties with partial derivatives, combining errors in quadrature,
least-squares fitting of a line of best fit, and the formal SI realisations of the
base units belong to A-level or university work and are not examined here.
