## What the syllabus requires

Define angular velocity and relate it to linear speed. Explain centripetal
acceleration and centripetal force, and calculate them. Give examples of circular
motion such as a conical pendulum, a car on a banked track and a satellite in
orbit. State Newton's law of universal gravitation and distinguish mass from
weight.

## Angular velocity and linear speed

A body moving in a circle of radius r sweeps out an angle. The angular velocity
omega is the angle swept per second, in radians per second. For one complete
revolution the angle is 2 pi radians, so

omega = 2 pi divided by T = 2 pi f,

where T is the period in seconds and f is the frequency in hertz. The linear speed
along the circle is v = omega r.

A wheel turning at 5 revolutions per second has f = 5 Hz and
omega = 2 pi (5) = 31.4 rad/s. A point 0.2 m from the axis moves at
v = 31.4 (0.2) = 6.28 m/s.

## Centripetal acceleration and force

A body moving at constant speed around a circle is still accelerating, because its
direction, and therefore its velocity, is changing. That acceleration points
towards the centre and has magnitude

a = v squared divided by r = omega squared times r.

The force producing it is the centripetal force, F = m v squared divided by r. It
is not a new kind of force: it is whatever real force happens to point at the
centre, such as the tension in a string, friction between tyre and road, or the
gravitational pull on a satellite.

A 2 kg mass whirled on a string of length 0.5 m at 4 m/s needs a centripetal force
of 2(16)/0.5 = 64 N, which is the tension in the string. If the string can only
stand 50 N it will break.

There is no outward force on the body. The outward push felt by a passenger in a
turning car is the seat pushing inward on the passenger and the passenger pushing
back on the seat, which is Newton's third law, not a centrifugal force on the
passenger.

## Gravitation, mass and weight

Newton's law of universal gravitation states that two bodies of masses M and m
whose centres are a distance r apart attract each other with a force

F = G M m divided by r squared,

where G is the universal gravitational constant. Near the surface of the Earth
this force is the weight of the body, W = mg.

Mass is the amount of matter in a body, a scalar, measured in kilograms, and it is
the same everywhere. Weight is the gravitational force on the body, a vector,
measured in newtons, and it changes with g. A 50 kg student weighs 50(10) = 500 N
on Earth and about 83 N on the Moon, where g is roughly one sixth as large, but
the mass is 50 kg in both places.

For a satellite in a circular orbit the gravitational force supplies the
centripetal force, so G M m divided by r squared equals m v squared divided by r,
which gives v squared = G M divided by r. The mass of the satellite cancels.

## Not on this syllabus

Rotational dynamics using moment of inertia, torque and angular momentum, the
derivation of Kepler's laws by calculus, the Coriolis effect, and the mechanics of
non-uniform circular motion with tangential acceleration are not examined here.
