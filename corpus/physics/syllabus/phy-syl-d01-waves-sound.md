## What the syllabus requires

Define a wave and distinguish transverse from longitudinal and progressive from
stationary waves. Define amplitude, wavelength, frequency, period and speed, and
use the wave equation. List the properties common to all waves. Describe the
production and transmission of sound, the factors affecting the speed of sound,
echoes, and the characteristics of a musical note. Describe resonance in air
columns and in stretched strings.

## Waves and the wave equation

A wave is a disturbance that transfers energy from one point to another without
transferring the material of the medium.

In a transverse wave the particles vibrate at right angles to the direction of
travel: water waves, waves on a rope, and all electromagnetic waves including
light. In a longitudinal wave the particles vibrate along the direction of travel,
producing compressions and rarefactions: sound is the standard example.

A mechanical wave needs a material medium; sound cannot travel through a vacuum.
Electromagnetic waves need no medium and travel through vacuum at
3 times 10 to the power 8 m/s.

The amplitude is the maximum displacement from the rest position. The wavelength
is the distance between two successive points in phase, such as two crests. The
frequency f is the number of complete waves passing a point per second, in hertz.
The period T is the time for one complete wave, and T = 1/f. These are linked by
the wave equation

v = f times lambda.

A wave of frequency 500 Hz and wavelength 0.68 m travels at
500(0.68) = 340 m/s, and its period is 1/500 = 0.002 s.

All waves can be reflected, refracted, diffracted and made to interfere. Only
transverse waves can be polarised, which is the evidence that light is transverse.

## Sound

Sound is produced by a vibrating body and travels as a longitudinal wave through a
material medium. It travels fastest in solids, more slowly in liquids and slowest
in gases, because the particles of a solid are closest together. In air it travels
at roughly 330 to 340 m/s, and the speed rises with temperature and with humidity
but does not depend on the pressure of the air or on the loudness of the sound.

An echo is sound reflected from a distant surface. If the echo returns after a
time t and the speed of sound is v, the sound has covered twice the distance d to
the reflector, so 2d = vt. A clap whose echo returns after 3 s from a cliff with
v = 340 m/s gives d = 340(3)/2 = 510 m.

The three characteristics of a musical note are pitch, which depends on frequency;
loudness, which depends on amplitude; and quality or timbre, which depends on the
number and strength of the overtones present and is what makes the same note
sound different on a flute and on a violin. Noise differs from a musical note in
having an irregular, non-repeating waveform.

## Resonance in pipes and strings

Resonance occurs when a body is made to vibrate at its own natural frequency by a
periodic force of that frequency, giving a large amplitude.

In a pipe closed at one end there is a node at the closed end and an antinode at
the open end, so the fundamental wavelength is four times the length of the pipe
and the fundamental frequency is f = v divided by 4L. A closed pipe 0.25 m long
with v = 340 m/s has a fundamental frequency of 340 divided by 1, that is 340 Hz.
A closed pipe produces only the odd harmonics.

In a pipe open at both ends there is an antinode at each end, the fundamental
wavelength is twice the length, and f = v divided by 2L. An open pipe produces all
the harmonics, so it sounds richer.

For a stretched string of length L, tension T and mass per unit length m, the
fundamental frequency is f = (1 divided by 2L) times the square root of (T divided
by m). The frequency rises if the string is shortened, tightened, or made lighter,
which is how a guitar and a sonometer are tuned.

## Not on this syllabus

The wave equation written as a partial differential equation, Fourier analysis of
a waveform, acoustic impedance, the decibel scale used quantitatively, and the
