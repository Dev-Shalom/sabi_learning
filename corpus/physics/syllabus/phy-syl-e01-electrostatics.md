## What the syllabus requires

Describe how a body is charged by friction, by contact and by induction, and use
the gold-leaf electroscope. State Coulomb's law and use it. Define electric field
intensity and electric potential. Define capacitance, calculate the combined
capacitance of capacitors in series and in parallel, and find the energy stored in
a charged capacitor. Describe the lightning conductor.

## Charge and charging

There are two kinds of charge, positive and negative. Like charges repel and
unlike charges attract, and repulsion is the only certain test for charge, because
a charged body also attracts an uncharged one by induction.

Charging by friction transfers electrons between two insulators rubbed together:
rubbing a polythene rod with fur leaves the rod negative because the rod gains
electrons. Charging by contact gives a body the same sign of charge as the charging
body. Charging by induction gives it the opposite sign: bring the charged body
near, earth the far side of the conductor momentarily, remove the earth, then
remove the charged body.

A gold-leaf electroscope detects charge and tests its sign. A charged body brought
near the cap makes the leaf diverge; if the leaf of an already charged electroscope
diverges further, the incoming charge has the same sign as the charge on the
electroscope.

Charge is measured in coulombs and is conserved: it is never created or destroyed,
only transferred.

## Coulomb's law and the electric field

Coulomb's law states that the force between two point charges is proportional to
the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the
distance between them:

F = k Q1 Q2 divided by r squared,

with k about 9 times 10 to the power 9 N m squared per coulomb squared in air. Two
charges of 2 microcoulomb and 3 microcoulomb 0.3 m apart repel with a force of
9 times 10 to the power 9 times 6 times 10 to the power -12 divided by 0.09, which
is 0.054/0.09 = 0.6 N.

The electric field intensity at a point is the force per unit positive charge
placed there, E = F/Q, in newtons per coulomb or volts per metre. For a point
charge, E = k Q divided by r squared. Field lines start on positive charge and end
on negative charge, never cross, and are closest together where the field is
strongest.

The electric potential at a point is the work done in bringing unit positive
charge from infinity to that point, V = kQ/r, in volts. The potential difference
between two points is the work done per unit charge in moving between them, and
W = QV.

## Capacitors

A capacitor stores charge. Its capacitance is the charge stored per volt,

C = Q/V,

in farads, where 1 F = 1 coulomb per volt. The farad is very large, so practical
values are in microfarad or picofarad. The capacitance of a parallel-plate
capacitor increases with the area of the plates and with the permittivity of the
dielectric between them, and decreases as the plates are moved apart.

Capacitors in parallel add directly: C = C1 + C2. Capacitors in series add as
reciprocals: 1/C = 1/C1 + 1/C2. Note that this is the opposite way round from
resistors. A 2 microfarad and a 3 microfarad capacitor give 5 microfarad in
parallel and (2 times 3) divided by (2 + 3) = 1.2 microfarad in series.

The energy stored in a charged capacitor is

E = one half C V squared = one half Q V = Q squared divided by 2C.

A 5 microfarad capacitor charged to 100 V stores
one half times 5 times 10 to the power -6 times 10000 = 0.025 J.

## Applications

A lightning conductor is a thick copper strip running from a spike above the
building to a plate buried in moist earth. Charge concentrates at the sharp point,
the strong field there ionises the air, and the charge leaks away quietly, while
any strike that does occur is carried safely to earth.

Other applications of electrostatics include the Van de Graaff generator, the
electrostatic precipitator that removes smoke particles from flue gases,
electrostatic spray painting, and the photocopier. The hazards include sparks from
fuel tankers, which is why they are earthed before unloading.
