## What the syllabus requires

Distinguish scalar from vector quantities and give examples of each. Find the
resultant of two or more coplanar vectors by the parallelogram law or by
resolution. Resolve a vector into two perpendicular components. State the
conditions for the equilibrium of coplanar forces and use them, including the
principle of moments.

## Scalars and vectors

A scalar has magnitude only: mass, time, distance, speed, energy, work, power,
temperature, volume, density, electric charge, potential difference.

A vector has magnitude and direction: displacement, velocity, acceleration, force,
weight, momentum, impulse, electric field intensity, magnetic flux density.

Two scalars add arithmetically. Two vectors do not: 3 N and 4 N give 7 N only if
they point the same way, 1 N if they oppose, and 5 N if they are perpendicular.

## Finding a resultant

For two vectors P and Q with an angle theta between them, the parallelogram law
gives the resultant R by

R squared = P squared + Q squared + 2 P Q cos theta.

The angle alpha that R makes with P is given by
tan alpha = Q sin theta divided by (P + Q cos theta).

For two forces of 5 N and 12 N at right angles, cos 90 = 0, so
R squared = 25 + 144 = 169 and R = 13 N. The angle to the 5 N force is
tan alpha = 12/5 = 2.4, so alpha = 67.4 degrees.

For forces of 6 N and 8 N at 60 degrees, R squared = 36 + 64 + 2(6)(8)(0.5) = 148,
so R = 12.2 N.

## Resolving a vector

A vector F at an angle theta to the horizontal has a horizontal component
F cos theta and a vertical component F sin theta. These two act exactly as the
single vector does, and they are independent of each other.

A force of 20 N at 60 degrees to the horizontal has a horizontal component
20 cos 60 = 10 N and a vertical component 20 sin 60 = 17.3 N.

To add several vectors, resolve each into horizontal and vertical components, add
the horizontal components to get X, add the vertical components to get Y, then
R squared = X squared + Y squared and tan theta = Y/X.

## Equilibrium of coplanar forces

A body is in equilibrium when the resultant force on it is zero and the resultant
moment about any point is zero. In component form, the sum of the horizontal
components is zero and the sum of the vertical components is zero.

If three forces keep a body in equilibrium, they can be drawn head to tail as a
closed triangle. That triangle of forces is often the quickest route to an unknown
force in a question about a body hanging from two strings.

The moment of a force about a point is force times perpendicular distance, in
newton metres. The principle of moments says that for a body in equilibrium the
sum of the clockwise moments about any point equals the sum of the anticlockwise
moments. A uniform rod pivoted at its centre carries 30 N at 0.4 m on one side;
the balancing force at 0.6 m on the other side is 30(0.4)/0.6 = 20 N.

## Not on this syllabus

The vector cross product and the scalar triple product, unit-vector algebra in
three dimensions, and vector calculus such as gradient, divergence and curl are
beyond this paper.
