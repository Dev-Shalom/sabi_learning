## What the syllabus requires

Define heat capacity and specific heat capacity and use them in calculations,
including the method of mixtures and electrical heating. Define specific latent
heat of fusion and of vaporisation and use them. Explain melting, boiling,
evaporation, and the effect of pressure and of impurities on the melting and
boiling points.

## Heat capacity and specific heat capacity

The heat capacity C of a body is the quantity of heat needed to raise its
temperature by one kelvin, in joules per kelvin: C = Q divided by delta theta.

The specific heat capacity c of a substance is the quantity of heat needed to
raise the temperature of one kilogram of it by one kelvin, in J/kg/K. So

Q = m c delta theta.

Water has a high specific heat capacity, 4200 J/kg/K, which is why it is used as a
coolant in car radiators and why the sea warms and cools more slowly than the land.

Heating 2 kg of water from 25 to 75 degrees Celsius needs
Q = 2(4200)(50) = 420000 J. If a 1400 W heater supplies it with no loss, the time
taken is 420000/1400 = 300 s, that is 5 minutes.

For 0.5 kg of aluminium with c = 900 J/kg/K raised by 50 K,
Q = 0.5(900)(50) = 22500 J.

## The method of mixtures

When a hot body is put into a cooler liquid and no heat escapes, the heat lost by
the hot body equals the heat gained by the cold liquid and its container:

heat lost by the hot body = heat gained by the liquid + heat gained by the
calorimeter.

Writing m1 c1 (theta1 - theta) = m2 c2 (theta - theta2) + mc cc (theta - theta2)
and solving for the one unknown is the standard experiment for finding an unknown
specific heat capacity. Heat losses to the surroundings are the main source of
error, and they are reduced by lagging the calorimeter, using a lid and a polished
outer surface, and starting a few degrees below room temperature.

## Latent heat

Latent heat is the heat absorbed or given out when a substance changes state at
constant temperature. The temperature does not change during the change of state
because the energy goes into separating the particles against the forces between
them, not into speeding them up.

The specific latent heat of fusion is the heat needed to change one kilogram of a
solid into liquid at its melting point, with no change of temperature. For ice it
is about 336000 J/kg. Melting 0.2 kg of ice at 0 degrees Celsius therefore needs
0.2(336000) = 67200 J.

The specific latent heat of vaporisation is the heat needed to change one kilogram
of a liquid into vapour at its boiling point. For water it is about
2260000 J/kg, which is far larger than the latent heat of fusion, because the
particles must be separated completely and work must be done pushing back the
atmosphere. This is why steam at 100 degrees Celsius scalds far worse than water
at 100 degrees Celsius.

The general heat formula for a problem with a change of state is
Q = m L for each change of state plus m c delta theta for each temperature change,
added together.

## Melting, boiling and evaporation

Evaporation happens at all temperatures and only at the surface of a liquid;
boiling happens throughout the liquid at one fixed temperature. Evaporation is
increased by a higher temperature, a larger surface area, a draught, and dry air,
and it produces cooling because the fastest molecules leave and the average energy
of those left behind falls. This is the principle of the refrigerator and of
sweating.

Increasing the pressure raises the boiling point, which is how a pressure cooker
works, and it lowers the melting point of ice, which is the basis of regelation.
Dissolving an impurity such as salt raises the boiling point of water and lowers
the melting point of ice, which is why salt is spread on icy roads.
