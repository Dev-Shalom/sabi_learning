## What the syllabus requires

Describe the path of a projectile and explain why the horizontal and vertical
motions are independent. Calculate the time of flight, the maximum height and the
range of a projectile launched at an angle, and the time and horizontal distance
for a body projected horizontally. State the angle that gives the greatest range.

## Why the two motions separate

Once a projectile has left the hand or the gun, the only force on it (ignoring air
resistance) is its weight, which acts vertically downwards. There is no horizontal
force, so the horizontal velocity stays constant for the whole flight. The
vertical motion is free fall with a = g = 10 m/s squared downwards.

So treat the problem as two separate straight-line problems that share one clock.
The horizontal equation is simply distance = horizontal velocity times time. The
vertical equations are the four equations of uniformly accelerated motion with
a = g. The path traced out is a parabola.

## Projection at an angle

A body projected with speed u at an angle theta to the horizontal has components
u cos theta horizontally and u sin theta vertically.

Time to reach the highest point = u sin theta divided by g.
Total time of flight T = 2 u sin theta divided by g.
Maximum height H = (u sin theta) squared divided by 2g.
Horizontal range R = u squared times sin 2 theta, divided by g.

At the highest point the vertical velocity is zero but the horizontal velocity is
still u cos theta, so the speed there is u cos theta, not zero.

Take u = 30 m/s at 30 degrees. Then u sin theta = 15 m/s and u cos theta = 26.0 m/s.
T = 2(15)/10 = 3 s. H = 225/20 = 11.25 m. R = 900 sin 60 divided by 10, which is
900(0.866)/10 = 77.9 m, and this agrees with 26.0 times 3.

The range is greatest when sin 2 theta = 1, that is at theta = 45 degrees. Two
angles that add up to 90 degrees, such as 30 and 60 degrees, give the same range.

## Horizontal projection

A body projected horizontally with speed u from a height h has no initial vertical
velocity, so h = one half g t squared and the time of flight is the square root of
2h/g. That time does not depend on u: a bullet fired horizontally and a bullet
dropped from the same height reach the ground together.

The horizontal distance covered is u times that time. For u = 20 m/s and h = 20 m,
t is the square root of 4, which is 2 s, and the horizontal distance is 40 m. The
vertical velocity on landing is gt = 20 m/s, so the resultant speed is the square
root of (400 + 400), which is 28.3 m/s.

## Not on this syllabus

Projectile motion with air resistance, drag proportional to speed or to speed
squared, the trajectory equation derived by calculus, ballistic corrections for a
rotating Earth, and orbital mechanics are not examined in this paper.
