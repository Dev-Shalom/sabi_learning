## What the syllabus requires

Distinguish heat from temperature. Name the thermometric properties used in the
common thermometers and convert between the Celsius and Kelvin scales. Define the
linear, area and volume expansivities and use them. Describe the effects of
expansion, the advantages and the problems it causes, and explain the anomalous
expansion of water. Describe conduction, convection and radiation.

## Heat and temperature

Temperature is the degree of hotness of a body, and it decides the direction in
which heat flows: heat flows from a body at a higher temperature to one at a lower
temperature until both are at the same temperature. Temperature is measured in
degrees Celsius or in kelvin.

Heat is a form of energy, measured in joules. Two bodies at the same temperature
can hold very different amounts of heat: a bucket of water at 40 degrees Celsius
contains far more heat than a cup of water at 40 degrees Celsius.

The Kelvin (absolute) scale is related to the Celsius scale by

T in kelvin = temperature in degrees Celsius + 273.

So 27 degrees Celsius is 300 K and 127 degrees Celsius is 400 K. Absolute zero,
0 K or -273 degrees Celsius, is the lowest temperature possible.

A thermometric property is a physical property that changes uniformly with
temperature: the length of a liquid column in a glass tube (liquid-in-glass
thermometer), the electrical resistance of a wire (resistance thermometer), the
e.m.f. of a junction of two metals (thermocouple), and the pressure or volume of a
fixed mass of gas (gas thermometer). Mercury is used for ordinary ranges because
it is opaque, expands uniformly and does not wet glass; alcohol is used for very
low temperatures because it freezes at a much lower temperature than mercury.

## The expansivities

Most solids expand when heated. For a rod of original length L and a temperature
rise of delta theta, the increase in length is

increase in length = L times alpha times delta theta,

where alpha is the linear expansivity of the material, in per kelvin (or per
degree Celsius, which is the same size of interval). Typical values are about
1.2 times 10 to the power -5 per K for steel and 1.9 times 10 to the power -5 per
K for brass.

A steel bridge girder 50 m long that warms by 30 degrees expands by
50 times 1.2 times 10 to the power -5 times 30 = 0.018 m, that is 1.8 cm, which is
why expansion gaps and roller bearings are built in.

The area expansivity is 2 alpha and the volume (cubic) expansivity is 3 alpha for
the same material. A brass sheet with alpha = 1.9 times 10 to the power -5 per K
has a volume expansivity of 5.7 times 10 to the power -5 per K.

## Effects of expansion and the anomaly of water

Expansion is useful in the bimetallic strip, where two metals of different linear
expansivity are riveted together so that the strip bends on heating; this is used
in a fire alarm, a thermostat and a flashing indicator. It is also used in rivets,
which are fitted hot and grip tightly on cooling, and in fitting a metal tyre onto
a wheel.

Expansion causes problems too: railway lines buckle, telephone wires sag, and
concrete roads crack, so gaps are left in each case.

Water is anomalous. Between 0 and 4 degrees Celsius water contracts as it is
heated, and it expands above 4 degrees Celsius. Water therefore has its maximum
density at 4 degrees Celsius. This is why ice floats and why a pond freezes from
the top down, leaving liquid water at 4 degrees Celsius at the bottom in which
fish survive. It is also why water pipes burst in very cold weather.

## Heat transfer

Conduction is the transfer of heat through a material without movement of the
material itself, by the vibration of particles and, in metals, by free electrons.
Metals are good conductors; wood, air, water and plastic are poor conductors.

Convection is the transfer of heat in a fluid by the actual movement of the fluid:
the warmed part expands, becomes less dense and rises, and cooler fluid takes its
place. Land and sea breezes, domestic hot-water systems and ventilation all work
by convection.

Radiation is the transfer of heat as electromagnetic waves, and it needs no
material medium; this is how heat reaches the Earth from the Sun. A dull black
surface is the best absorber and emitter; a shiny silvered surface is the best
reflector and the poorest emitter, which is why a vacuum flask is silvered.

## Not on this syllabus

Entropy and the formal laws of thermodynamics, the Carnot cycle, Fourier's law of
conduction with a temperature gradient and calculus, the Stefan-Boltzmann and Wien
laws used quantitatively, and statistical mechanics are not examined here.
