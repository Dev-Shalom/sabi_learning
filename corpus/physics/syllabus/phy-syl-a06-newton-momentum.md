## What the syllabus requires

State Newton's three laws of motion and use them. Define linear momentum and
impulse and relate them. State the principle of conservation of linear momentum
and apply it to collisions and explosions in one dimension. Distinguish elastic
from inelastic collisions. Describe friction and state its effects.

## Newton's three laws

The first law: a body stays at rest, or keeps moving in a straight line at
constant speed, unless a resultant external force acts on it. This is the law of
inertia, and inertia is measured by mass.

The second law: the rate of change of momentum of a body is proportional to the
resultant force and takes place in the direction of that force. For a constant
mass this reduces to F = ma, with F in newtons when m is in kilograms and a is in
m/s squared. One newton is the force that gives a mass of 1 kg an acceleration of
1 m/s squared.

The third law: to every action there is an equal and opposite reaction. The two
forces are equal in magnitude, opposite in direction, of the same kind, and act on
two different bodies, which is why they never cancel each other out.

A resultant force of 12 N on a 4 kg body gives a = 12/4 = 3 m/s squared. If a
friction force of 4 N also acts, the resultant is 8 N and a = 2 m/s squared.

## Momentum and impulse

The linear momentum of a body is p = mv, in kg m/s, and it is a vector. A 3 kg
body moving at 4 m/s has a momentum of 12 kg m/s in the direction of motion.

Impulse is force times the time for which it acts, Ft, in newton seconds, and it
equals the change of momentum:

Ft = mv - mu.

This is why a longer contact time means a smaller force for the same change of
momentum, which is the reason for crumple zones, air bags, and bending the knees
on landing. A 0.2 kg ball brought from 15 m/s to rest in 0.05 s experiences an
average force of 0.2(15)/0.05 = 60 N.

## Conservation of linear momentum

When no external force acts on a system, its total momentum stays constant. For
two bodies colliding in a straight line,

m1 u1 + m2 u2 = m1 v1 + m2 v2.

Give each velocity a sign according to its direction before substituting.

In a perfectly inelastic collision the bodies stick together and move with a common
velocity. A 2 kg body at 6 m/s striking a stationary 4 kg body and sticking to it
gives a common velocity of 12/6 = 2 m/s.

Momentum is conserved in every collision. Kinetic energy is conserved only in a
perfectly elastic collision. In an inelastic collision the missing kinetic energy
becomes heat, sound and permanent deformation.

An explosion is the same principle run backwards: a stationary object with zero
total momentum splits, and the fragments must carry equal and opposite momenta.
This is also how a rocket and a recoiling gun work.

## Friction

Friction is the force that opposes relative motion between two surfaces in
contact. Limiting friction, the largest value before sliding starts, is
proportional to the normal reaction: F = mu R, where mu is the coefficient of
friction. On a horizontal surface R = mg, so for a 5 kg block with mu = 0.4 the
limiting friction is 0.4(5)(10) = 20 N.

Friction wastes energy as heat and wears surfaces, and it is reduced by
lubrication, ball bearings and streamlining. It is also essential: without it
walking, writing, braking and the use of belt drives would be impossible.

## Not on this syllabus

Variable-mass problems such as the rocket equation, relativistic momentum, the
work-energy theorem derived by integration, and multi-body collision problems in
two dimensions requiring simultaneous vector equations are not examined here.
