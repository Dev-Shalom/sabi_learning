## What the syllabus requires

Describe magnets, magnetic fields and the magnetic field of the Earth. Calculate
the force on a current-carrying conductor and on a moving charge in a magnetic
field, and apply Fleming's left-hand rule. Describe the moving-coil galvanometer
and the electric motor. State Faraday's laws and Lenz's law and use them. Describe
the a.c. generator and the transformer, and explain the transmission of electrical
power. Distinguish peak from root-mean-square values in an a.c. circuit and
calculate reactance, impedance and resonance.

## Magnetic fields and the force on a conductor

A magnetic field is the region around a magnet or a current in which a magnetic
force acts. Field lines run from the north pole to the south pole outside the
magnet, never cross, and are closest where the field is strongest. Magnetic flux
density B is measured in teslas, and the magnetic flux through an area A
perpendicular to the field is Phi = B A, in webers.

A straight conductor of length L carrying a current I at an angle theta to a field
of flux density B experiences a force

F = B I L sin theta,

which is greatest when the conductor is perpendicular to the field and zero when
it lies along the field. A conductor 0.2 m long carrying 4 A perpendicular to a
0.5 T field experiences 0.5(4)(0.2) = 0.4 N.

A charge Q moving with speed v at an angle theta to the field experiences
F = B Q v sin theta. Because this force is always perpendicular to the velocity,
it changes the direction but not the speed of the charge, so a charged particle
entering a uniform field at right angles moves in a circle.

Fleming's left-hand rule gives the direction of the force on a current in a field:
hold the thumb, first finger and second finger of the left hand mutually at right
angles, with the first finger along the field and the second finger along the
current; the thumb then points along the force or motion.

The moving-coil galvanometer and the direct-current electric motor both depend on
this force: a current-carrying coil in a radial magnetic field experiences a
couple that turns it. In the motor a split-ring commutator reverses the current
every half turn so that the couple keeps acting the same way round.

## Electromagnetic induction

Faraday's first law states that whenever the magnetic flux linking a circuit
changes, an electromotive force is induced in that circuit. His second law states
that the magnitude of the induced electromotive force is proportional to the rate
of change of flux linkage, so the magnitude is

E = N times (change in flux) divided by (time taken),

where N is the number of turns.

Lenz's law states that the induced current flows in such a direction as to oppose
the change producing it. It is a statement of the conservation of energy: if the
induced current helped the change, energy would be created from nothing. This is
why work must be done to push a magnet into a coil.

Fleming's right-hand rule gives the direction of the induced current in a
conductor moved through a field, using the same finger arrangement as the
left-hand rule but with the right hand.

In an a.c. generator a coil is rotated in a magnetic field; the flux linkage
changes continuously and the induced electromotive force reverses every half turn,
giving alternating current, which is taken off through slip rings.

## Transformers, transmission and a.c. circuits

A transformer changes an alternating voltage. For an ideal transformer

Vs divided by Vp = Ns divided by Np,

where p and s stand for primary and secondary. A step-up transformer has more
secondary turns; a step-down transformer has fewer. For an ideal transformer the
power out equals the power in, so Vp Ip = Vs Is. A transformer works on
alternating current only, because a steady current produces no change of flux.

Energy is lost in a real transformer by heating in the windings (reduced by using
thick copper wire), by eddy currents in the core (reduced by laminating the core),
by hysteresis (reduced by using soft iron), and by flux leakage (reduced by
winding both coils on the same limb).

Electrical power is transmitted at very high voltage and therefore low current,
because the power wasted as heat in the cables is I squared R, and halving the
current cuts that loss to a quarter. Step-up transformers raise the voltage at the
station and step-down transformers lower it again for the consumer.

In an alternating-current circuit the peak value and the root-mean-square value
are related by V rms = V peak divided by the square root of 2. A mains supply of
peak value 311 V has an r.m.s. value of 311/1.414 = 220 V, and it is the r.m.s.
value that meters and ratings quote.

The inductive reactance of a coil is XL = 2 pi f L and the capacitive reactance of
a capacitor is XC = 1 divided by (2 pi f C), both in ohms. The impedance of a
series circuit containing R, L and C is

Z = the square root of (R squared + (XL - XC) squared),

and V = I Z. At resonance XL equals XC, the impedance falls to R, the current is a
maximum, and the resonant frequency is f = 1 divided by (2 pi times the square
root of LC).
