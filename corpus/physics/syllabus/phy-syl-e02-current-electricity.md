## What the syllabus requires

Define electric current, potential difference and electromotive force. State
Ohm's law and its conditions. Calculate the resistance of conductors in series and
in parallel and use the resistivity formula. Account for internal resistance and
terminal potential difference. Calculate electrical energy and power and the cost
of electricity. Describe the ammeter, the voltmeter, the metre bridge and the
potentiometer.

## Current, potential difference and Ohm's law

Electric current is the rate of flow of charge, I = Q/t, in amperes, where one
ampere is one coulomb per second. Conventional current flows from the positive to
the negative terminal outside the cell, which is opposite to the direction in
which electrons actually drift.

Potential difference between two points is the work done per unit charge in moving
charge between them, in volts. Electromotive force is the total work done per unit
charge by the source, including the work done inside the source itself.

Ohm's law states that the current through a metallic conductor is directly
proportional to the potential difference across its ends, provided the temperature
and other physical conditions stay constant. So V = IR, where R is the resistance
in ohms. A conductor that obeys this law has a straight-line V-against-I graph
through the origin; a filament lamp, a diode and an electrolyte do not obey it.

The resistance of a uniform conductor is

R = rho L divided by A,

where rho is the resistivity in ohm metres, L the length and A the
cross-sectional area. Resistance rises with length, falls with cross-sectional
area, and for a metal rises with temperature. A wire of resistivity
1.1 times 10 to the power -6 ohm metre, length 2 m and area
5.5 times 10 to the power -7 square metres has
R = 2.2 times 10 to the power -6 divided by 5.5 times 10 to the power -7 = 4 ohms.

## Resistors in series and in parallel

In series the same current flows through each resistor and the potential
differences add, so

R = R1 + R2 + R3.

In parallel the same potential difference is across each resistor and the currents
add, so

1/R = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3.

For two resistors in parallel the combined resistance is the product divided by
the sum. A 4 ohm and a 6 ohm resistor give 10 ohms in series and
24/10 = 2.4 ohms in parallel. The parallel value is always smaller than the
smallest resistor in the group.

## Internal resistance, energy and power

A cell of electromotive force E and internal resistance r driving a current I
through an external resistance R satisfies

E = I (R + r), and the terminal potential difference is V = E - I r.

The term I r is the lost volts, the energy per coulomb used up inside the cell
itself. A 12 V cell of internal resistance 0.5 ohm supplying a 5.5 ohm resistor
drives I = 12/6 = 2 A and its terminal potential difference is
12 - 2(0.5) = 11 V.

The electrical energy converted in a resistor is W = V I t = I squared R t =
V squared t divided by R, in joules, and the power is P = V I = I squared R = V
squared divided by R, in watts. For billing, energy is measured in kilowatt-hours:
one kilowatt-hour is the energy used by a 1000 W appliance in one hour, that is
3.6 times 10 to the power 6 J. A 1000 W iron used for 3 hours a day for 30 days
uses 90 kWh.

## Measuring instruments

An ammeter measures current and is connected in series. It must have a very low
resistance so that it hardly changes the current it is measuring. A galvanometer
is converted into an ammeter by connecting a low resistance, the shunt, in
parallel with it.

A voltmeter measures potential difference and is connected in parallel with the
component. It must have a very high resistance so that it draws almost no current.
A galvanometer is converted into a voltmeter by connecting a high resistance, the
multiplier, in series with it.

The metre bridge is a practical form of the Wheatstone bridge and is used to
compare two resistances: at balance, when the galvanometer reads zero, the ratio
of the two resistances equals the ratio of the two lengths of the bridge wire.

The potentiometer compares potential differences or electromotive forces by
balancing them against the potential difference along a uniform wire, and because
it draws no current at balance it measures the electromotive force of a cell
rather than its terminal potential difference.
