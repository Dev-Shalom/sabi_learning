## What the syllabus requires

Define the mole, the Avogadro constant and molar mass. Convert between mass,
moles, number of particles and volume of gas at s.t.p. Write and balance chemical
equations. Determine empirical and molecular formulae from composition data. Use
a balanced equation to calculate reacting masses and volumes, identify the
limiting reactant, and calculate percentage purity and percentage yield.

## The mole and the four conversions

A mole is the amount of substance that contains as many elementary entities as
there are atoms in 12 g of carbon-12. That number is the Avogadro constant,
6.02 x 10^23 per mole.

Four conversions do most of the work:

1. Moles from mass: number of moles = mass in grams divided by molar mass in
   grams per mole.
2. Moles from particles: number of moles = number of particles divided by
   6.02 x 10^23.
3. Moles from volume of gas at s.t.p.: number of moles = volume in dm3 divided by
   22.4 dm3 per mole. Standard temperature and pressure is 273 K and 760 mmHg.
4. Moles from solution: number of moles = concentration in mol/dm3 times volume
   in dm3.

Worked figure. The molar mass of oxygen gas, O2, is 2 x 16 = 32 g/mol. So 8 g of
oxygen is 8/32 = 0.25 mol. That is 0.25 x 6.02 x 10^23 = 1.505 x 10^23
molecules, and at s.t.p. it occupies 0.25 x 22.4 = 5.6 dm3.

Note which particle is being counted. One mole of O2 contains 6.02 x 10^23
molecules but 1.204 x 10^24 atoms, because each molecule holds two atoms.

## Empirical and molecular formulae

The empirical formula is the simplest whole-number ratio of the atoms in a
compound. The molecular formula is the actual number of each kind of atom in one
molecule, and it is always a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula.

To find an empirical formula from percentage composition: divide each percentage
by the relative atomic mass of that element, then divide all the results by the
smallest, then round to whole numbers.

Worked figure. A compound is 40 per cent carbon, 6.7 per cent hydrogen and
53.3 per cent oxygen. Dividing by relative atomic masses gives
40/12 = 3.33, 6.7/1 = 6.7, 53.3/16 = 3.33. Dividing by the smallest, 3.33, gives
1 : 2 : 1, so the empirical formula is CH2O.

To get the molecular formula, divide the relative molecular mass by the empirical
formula mass. The mass of CH2O is 12 + 2 + 16 = 30. If the relative molecular
mass is 180, then 180/30 = 6 and the molecular formula is C6H12O6.

## Reacting masses, limiting reactant and yield

A balanced equation is a statement about moles. In

CaCO3 -> CaO + CO2

one mole of calcium trioxocarbonate(IV) gives one mole of calcium oxide and one
mole of carbon dioxide. So 25 g of CaCO3, which is 25/100 = 0.25 mol, gives
0.25 mol of CaO, that is 0.25 x 56 = 14 g, together with 0.25 x 22.4 = 5.6 dm3 of
carbon dioxide measured at s.t.p.

When the amounts of two reactants are both given, they are usually not in the
ratio the equation demands. The limiting reactant is the one that runs out first,
and it is the one that decides how much product forms. Convert both to moles,
divide each by its coefficient in the equation, and the smaller result is the
limiting reactant.

Percentage purity compares the mass of pure substance found with the mass of the
impure sample: purity = (mass of pure substance / mass of sample) x 100.

Percentage yield compares what was actually obtained with what the equation says
should be obtained: yield = (actual yield / theoretical yield) x 100. It is below
100 per cent because of side reactions, incomplete reaction, and losses in
filtering and transferring.

## Not on this syllabus

Rate equations and orders of reaction, atom economy as a defined quantity,
back-titration treated as a standard method, the ideal gas equation PV = nRT with
the gas constant R, and calculations using non-standard molar volumes other than
the 22.4 dm3 at s.t.p. and the approximate 24 dm3 at room conditions are not
examined here.
