## What the syllabus requires

Define an acid and a base both in terms of ions in water and in terms of proton
transfer. Distinguish strong from weak and concentrated from dilute. State the
basicity of an acid. Classify oxides as acidic, basic, amphoteric or neutral.
Name the types of salt and describe the methods of preparing them. Use the pH
scale and explain the hydrolysis of salts.

## Definitions

On the older definition, an acid is a substance that produces hydrogen ions as
the only positive ions when dissolved in water, and a base is a substance that
reacts with an acid to give a salt and water only. An alkali is a base that is
soluble in water and produces hydroxide ions.

On the Bronsted-Lowry definition, an acid is a proton donor and a base is a
proton acceptor. In

HCl + H2O -> H3O+ + Cl-

the hydrogen chloride donates a proton and the water accepts one, so here water
is acting as a base. In the reverse direction H3O+ donates and Cl- accepts, so
Cl- is the conjugate base of HCl.

Strong and weak describe how completely an acid ionises in water. Hydrochloric,
trioxonitrate(V) and tetraoxosulphate(VI) acids are strong: they ionise almost
completely. Ethanoic and trioxocarbonate(IV) acids are weak: only a small
fraction of the molecules ionise, and the ionisation is reversible. Concentrated
and dilute describe how much acid there is in a given volume of solution, which
is a different question entirely: concentrated ethanoic acid is still a weak
acid.

Basicity is the number of replaceable hydrogen ions per molecule of the acid.
HCl is monobasic, H2SO4 is dibasic and H3PO4 is tribasic. Basicity fixes the mole
ratio in a neutralisation: one mole of H2SO4 needs two moles of NaOH.

## Oxides and the pH scale

An acidic oxide is the oxide of a non-metal that reacts with a base to give a
salt and water, such as CO2, SO2, SO3 and P4O10. Those that dissolve give acidic
solutions.

A basic oxide is the oxide of a metal that reacts with an acid to give a salt and
water, such as Na2O, CaO, MgO and CuO. Those that dissolve give alkaline
solutions and are called alkalis.

An amphoteric oxide reacts with both acids and alkalis to give a salt and water.
Al2O3, ZnO and PbO are amphoteric. Zinc oxide gives a zinc salt with acid and a
tetraoxozincate(II) with excess alkali.

A neutral oxide reacts with neither. Water, carbon(II) oxide and nitrogen(I)
oxide are neutral oxides.

The pH scale runs from 0 to 14 and measures the hydrogen ion concentration.
pH = 7 is neutral, below 7 is acidic and above 7 is alkaline. The scale is
logarithmic: pH = -log of the hydrogen ion concentration in mol/dm3. A solution
with a hydrogen ion concentration of 1 x 10^-3 mol/dm3 has a pH of 3, and a
solution of 1 x 10^-5 mol/dm3 has a pH of 5, so the first is a hundred times more
acidic than the second.

## Salts and their preparation

A salt is formed when the replaceable hydrogen of an acid is replaced by a metal
or by the ammonium ion.

- A normal salt has all the replaceable hydrogen replaced: NaCl, Na2SO4, CaCO3.
- An acid salt has only part of it replaced, so it still has replaceable
  hydrogen: NaHCO3, NaHSO4. Only an acid of basicity two or more can form one.
- A basic salt contains hydroxide or oxide as well as the acid radical, such as
  basic lead(II) trioxocarbonate(IV).
- A double salt is formed by crystallising two simple salts together and gives
  the ions of both in solution, such as potassium aluminium tetraoxosulphate(VI),
  which is alum.

The method of preparation follows from solubility. A soluble salt of a reactive
metal is made by adding excess insoluble base, metal or carbonate to the acid,
filtering off the excess, and crystallising the filtrate. A soluble salt of a
sodium, potassium or ammonium compound must be made by titration, because the
base dissolves and no excess can be filtered off. An insoluble salt is made by
precipitation: mix solutions of two soluble salts that will exchange partners,
then filter, wash and dry the precipitate.

Which salts are soluble is worth remembering: all trioxonitrates(V) are soluble;
all sodium, potassium and ammonium salts are soluble; all chlorides are soluble
except those of silver and lead(II); all tetraoxosulphates(VI) are soluble except
those of barium, calcium and lead(II); all trioxocarbonates(IV) are insoluble
except those of sodium, potassium and ammonium.

## Hydrolysis of salts

A salt of a strong acid and a strong base, such as NaCl, dissolves to give a
neutral solution. A salt of a strong acid and a weak base, such as ammonium
chloride, hydrolyses to give an acidic solution. A salt of a weak acid and a
strong base, such as sodium ethanoate or sodium trioxocarbonate(IV), hydrolyses
to give an alkaline solution. That is why a solution of washing soda feels
alkaline although it contains no hydroxide as such.

## Not on this syllabus

Buffer solutions treated quantitatively by the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation,
acid and base dissociation constants Ka and Kb and calculations of the pH of weak
acids from them, the ionic product of water used in calculation, titration curve
sketching with calculated pH values, the Lewis definition of acids and bases, and
the solubility product are not examined here.
