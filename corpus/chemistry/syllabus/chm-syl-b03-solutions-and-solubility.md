## What the syllabus requires

Define solute, solvent, solution, saturated and unsaturated solution, and
solubility. Express concentration in mol/dm3 and in g/dm3 and convert between
them. Calculate the concentration of a diluted solution. Read and use a
solubility curve, including the mass of crystals deposited on cooling. Describe
the difference between a true solution, a colloid and a suspension.

## The vocabulary

A solute is the substance that dissolves; a solvent is the liquid it dissolves
in; a solution is the uniform mixture that results. A solution is dilute if it
holds little solute for the amount of solvent and concentrated if it holds much.

A saturated solution is one that cannot dissolve any more of the solute at that
temperature in the presence of undissolved solute. An unsaturated solution can
dissolve more. A supersaturated solution holds more than the saturation amount
and deposits the excess as soon as it is disturbed or seeded.

The solubility of a solute in a solvent at a given temperature is the maximum
amount of the solute that will dissolve in a stated amount of the solvent at that
temperature. It is quoted either as grams per 100 g of solvent or as moles per
dm3 of solution, and the temperature must always be stated because solubility
depends on it.

## Concentration, and converting between its units

Concentration in mol/dm3, the molar concentration, is the number of moles of
solute in one cubic decimetre of solution. Concentration in g/dm3, the mass
concentration, is the mass of solute in one cubic decimetre. They are linked by
the molar mass:

concentration in g/dm3 = concentration in mol/dm3 x molar mass

Remember that 1 dm3 = 1000 cm3, so a volume in cm3 is divided by 1000 before it
is used.

Worked figure. 5.3 g of anhydrous sodium trioxocarbonate(IV), Na2CO3, is
dissolved and made up to 250 cm3 of solution. The molar mass is
(2 x 23) + 12 + (3 x 16) = 46 + 12 + 48 = 106 g/mol, so the amount dissolved is
5.3/106 = 0.05 mol. The volume is 250/1000 = 0.25 dm3, so the concentration is
0.05/0.25 = 0.2 mol/dm3. In mass terms that is 0.2 x 106 = 21.2 g/dm3.

## Dilution

Diluting a solution adds solvent but does not change the number of moles of
solute. So for a dilution,

C1V1 = C2V2

where C1 and V1 are the concentration and volume before, and C2 and V2 after.

Worked figure. 25 cm3 of 2.0 mol/dm3 hydrochloric acid is made up to 500 cm3 with
water. The new concentration is C2 = (2.0 x 25) / 500 = 0.1 mol/dm3. The solution
has been diluted twenty-fold, and the concentration has fallen by the same
factor.

## Solubility curves and crystallisation

A solubility curve is a graph of solubility, usually in grams per 100 g of water,
against temperature. For most solids the curve rises, because dissolving is
usually endothermic; for calcium ethanoate and for a few other solutes it falls.
For gases, solubility falls as temperature rises and increases with pressure,
which is why a warm fizzy drink loses its gas faster.

Worked figure. A salt has a solubility of 40 g per 100 g of water at 60 degrees
Celsius and 20 g per 100 g of water at 20 degrees Celsius. A solution saturated
at 60 degrees Celsius and containing 100 g of water is cooled to 20 degrees
Celsius. The water can now hold only 20 g, so 40 - 20 = 20 g of the salt
crystallises out. That is the principle of purification by recrystallisation: the
wanted solid comes out of solution while the impurities, present in small
amounts, stay dissolved.

## True solutions, colloids and suspensions

In a true solution the particles are of molecular or ionic size, the mixture is
clear, it passes through filter paper, and it does not settle on standing.

In a suspension the particles are large enough to be seen, the mixture is cloudy,
it is held back by filter paper, and it settles on standing. Muddy water is a
suspension.

A colloid lies between the two. Its particles are too small to be filtered out or
to settle, but large enough to scatter a beam of light, which is the Tyndall
effect. Milk, starch solution, fog and foam are colloids.

## Not on this syllabus

Colligative properties treated quantitatively, that is elevation of boiling
point, depression of freezing point and osmotic pressure calculations; the
solubility product Ksp and the common-ion effect; partition coefficients and
solvent extraction calculations; and Raoult's law are not examined here.
