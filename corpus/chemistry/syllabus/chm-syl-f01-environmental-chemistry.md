## What the syllabus requires

State the composition of clean air. Name the common air pollutants, say where
each comes from and what harm it does, and explain acid rain, the greenhouse
effect and the depletion of the ozone layer in outline. Name the sources of
water pollution and their effects, and state how water is treated for domestic
use. Explain what hard water is, distinguish temporary from permanent hardness,
write the equations for how each arises and how each is removed, and state the
advantages and disadvantages of hard water. Name the important chemical
industries in West Africa, their raw materials and their products, and state the
benefits and the environmental costs of siting them.

## The composition of clean air

Clean dry air is a mixture, not a compound, of about 78 per cent nitrogen and 21
per cent oxygen by volume, with about 0.9 per cent argon and the other noble
gases and about 0.03 per cent carbon(IV) oxide. Water vapour and dust are
present in amounts that vary from place to place and from day to day.

That it is a mixture is shown by the fact that its components keep their own
properties, that the proportions vary slightly, and that it is separated by the
physical process of fractional distillation of liquefied air rather than by
chemical means.

## Air pollution

A pollutant is a substance present where it does harm. The ones to know are
these.

Carbon(II) oxide, CO, comes from the incomplete combustion of carbon fuels in a
limited supply of air, especially in vehicle engines and in a generator run in a
closed room. It is colourless and has no smell, and it is poisonous because it
combines with haemoglobin far more strongly than oxygen does, so the blood can
no longer carry oxygen.

Sulphur(IV) oxide, SO2, comes from burning fossil fuels that contain sulphur and
from roasting sulphide ores. It irritates the lungs, and in moist air it is
oxidised and dissolved to give acid rain.

The oxides of nitrogen, NO and NO2, are formed when nitrogen and oxygen combine
in the high temperature of an engine cylinder or a furnace, and by lightning.
They also give acid rain, and in strong sunlight they take part in forming
photochemical smog.

Carbon(IV) oxide, CO2, is not poisonous, but the amount in the air is rising as
fossil fuels are burnt and forests are cleared. It absorbs the heat radiated
from the warmed earth, which is the greenhouse effect, and the result is global
warming.

Also of concern are unburnt hydrocarbons and soot from engines and from gas
flaring; lead compounds from leaded petrol, which damage the nervous system;
chlorofluorocarbons from old refrigerators and aerosols, which drift up and
destroy the ozone layer that screens out ultraviolet light; and hydrogen
sulphide and ammonia from decaying matter.

Acid rain is rain made acidic by dissolved sulphur(IV) oxide and oxides of
nitrogen, which form trioxosulphate(IV), tetraoxosulphate(VI) and
trioxonitrate(V) acids. It corrodes buildings and statues made of limestone and
marble, because calcium trioxocarbonate(IV) reacts with acid; it rusts iron
roofing sheets; it makes soil and lake water too acidic for crops and for fish.
It is reduced by using fuels low in sulphur, by removing sulphur(IV) oxide from
flue gases with lime or limestone, by fitting catalytic converters to vehicle
exhausts, and by using unleaded petrol.

## Water pollution

Water is polluted by sewage and other untreated organic waste, which feeds
bacteria that use up the dissolved oxygen as they decay it, so that fish
suffocate and the water begins to smell. It is polluted by fertiliser washed off
farmland, whose trioxonitrate(V) and tetraoxophosphate(V) feed a sudden growth
of algae; when the algae die the same loss of dissolved oxygen follows. It is
polluted by industrial effluent carrying acids and the compounds of lead,
mercury and cadmium, which are poisonous and accumulate in fish. It is polluted
by crude oil from spillage and from tank washing, which spreads as a film that
keeps out air and light, kills fish and mangrove, and ruins farmland and
fishing grounds. And it is polluted by non-biodegradable detergents, which foam
on the surface, and by hot water from power stations, which lowers the amount
of oxygen the water can hold.

Water for domestic supply is treated by allowing suspended matter to settle,
with alum added to make fine particles clump together, then filtering through
sand and gravel, then chlorinating to kill bacteria. Aeration removes dissolved
gases that give a bad taste.

## Hard water

Hard water is water that does not readily lather with soap, giving instead a
grey solid called scum. The hardness is caused by dissolved calcium and
magnesium ions. Soap is the sodium salt of a long-chain alkanoic acid, and those
ions replace the sodium to give a calcium or magnesium salt that is insoluble:

2C17H35COONa + Ca2+ -> (C17H35COO)2Ca + 2Na+

The soap is wasted on the scum until every calcium and magnesium ion has been
taken out of solution, and only then does a lather form.

Temporary hardness is caused by the dissolved hydrogentrioxocarbonate(IV) salts,
Ca(HCO3)2 and Mg(HCO3)2. It arises when rain water, which carries dissolved
carbon(IV) oxide and so is weakly acidic, runs over limestone or dolomite:

CaCO3 + H2O + CO2 -> Ca(HCO3)2

Permanent hardness is caused by the dissolved tetraoxosulphate(VI) and chloride
salts of the same two metals: CaSO4, MgSO4, CaCl2 and MgCl2. It arises where the
rock contains gypsum or similar minerals.

Hard water is not all a nuisance. It tastes pleasant, it supplies calcium for
bones and teeth, and the coating it lays down inside lead pipes reduces lead
poisoning; brewing and tanning want it. Against that, it wastes soap; it furs
kettles, boilers and hot-water pipes with a scale that wastes fuel, can block
the pipe and can make a boiler unsafe; and it stains baths and basins.

## Softening hard water

Temporary hardness is removed by boiling, which decomposes the
hydrogentrioxocarbonate(IV) back to the insoluble trioxocarbonate(IV):

Ca(HCO3)2 -> CaCO3 + H2O + CO2

That precipitate is the scale in a kettle. Temporary hardness is also removed by
adding a calculated amount of slaked lime, calcium hydroxide, which is Clark's
method:

Ca(HCO3)2 + Ca(OH)2 -> 2CaCO3 + 2H2O

Adding too much simply makes the water hard again with calcium hydroxide, which
is why the amount must be calculated.

Permanent hardness is not affected by boiling. It is removed by adding washing
soda, sodium trioxocarbonate(IV), which precipitates the calcium as the
insoluble trioxocarbonate(IV) and leaves harmless sodium ions behind:

CaSO4 + Na2CO3 -> CaCO3 + Na2SO4

It is also removed by ion exchange, in which the water is passed through a
column of sodium permutite or a synthetic resin that holds sodium ions loosely
and exchanges them for the calcium and magnesium ions; the column is regenerated
by flushing it with brine. Distillation removes both kinds of hardness, since
the salts are left behind in the flask, but it is too expensive for ordinary
use. Washing soda, ion exchange and distillation all remove temporary hardness
as well as permanent.

Worked figure. One cubic decimetre of a hard water contains 0.162 g of
Ca(HCO3)2. With H = 1, C = 12, O = 16 and Ca = 40 the relative molecular mass is
40 + 2 x (1 + 12 + 48) = 162, so the sample holds 0.162/162 = 0.001 mol of
Ca(HCO3)2 per cubic decimetre. Since one cubic decimetre of water has a mass of
about 1 kg, that is 162 mg in 10 to the power 6 mg, or 162 parts per million of
Ca(HCO3)2, all of it temporary hardness and all of it removable by boiling.

## The chemical industry in West Africa

Petroleum refining is the largest chemical industry in the region. Crude oil
from the Niger Delta is separated by fractional distillation at refineries
including those at Port Harcourt, Warri and Kaduna, and at Tema in Ghana, into
refinery gas, petrol, kerosene, diesel and bitumen. Cracking and reforming
upgrade the heavier fractions, and the light fractions feed the petrochemical
plants that make the monomers for plastics and the raw materials for synthetic
fibres, detergents and solvents.

Natural gas that was once flared is now partly collected, liquefied for export
and used as feedstock. The ammonia and urea of the fertiliser plants are made
from it, by the Haber process and then by combining ammonia with carbon(IV)
oxide; blended NPK fertilisers are made alongside.

Cement is made by heating limestone with clay in a rotary kiln and grinding the
product with a little gypsum, at works sited on the limestone deposits. Other
established industries are soap and detergent manufacture from local palm oil
and imported alkali, paints, pharmaceuticals, breweries, plastics and glass, and
aluminium smelting from bauxite where cheap electricity is available.

They are sited where the raw material, the power and the transport are: crude
oil and natural gas in the Delta, limestone and clay inland, brine and salt beds
for the chlor-alkali industry, bauxite in Ghana and Guinea. The gains are jobs,
goods made at home instead of imported, and foreign exchange. The costs are the
gas flaring, the oil spillage, the effluent and the quarry dust described
earlier in this document, which is why siting, effluent treatment and
enforcement matter as much as the chemistry.

## Not on this syllabus

The quantitative treatment of pollutant dispersal is not examined here: plume
and dispersion models, the arithmetic of atmospheric residence times and mixing
ratios, radiative-forcing and climate-sensitivity calculations, and the
statutory air-quality limits of any particular country as numbers to be
recalled. Also outside this syllabus are the photochemical mechanisms of ozone
depletion and of smog formation step by step with radical intermediates, the
full determination of biochemical oxygen demand by the Winkler method as a
calculation, instrumental monitoring of pollutants such as atomic absorption
spectroscopy and gas chromatography, and the detailed plant design, costing and
economics of the industries named above.
