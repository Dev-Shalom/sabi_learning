## What the syllabus requires

Define electrolysis, electrolyte, electrode, anode and cathode. Explain
preferential discharge and predict the products of electrolysing a named
electrolyte with named electrodes. State Faraday's laws of electrolysis and use
the Faraday constant to calculate the mass of substance deposited or the volume
of gas liberated. Describe the industrial applications.

## The vocabulary

Electrolysis is the decomposition of a compound by the passage of an electric
current through it in the molten state or in aqueous solution.

An electrolyte is a substance that conducts electricity when molten or in
solution and is decomposed by it: ionic compounds, acids and alkalis. A
non-electrolyte, such as sugar solution, petrol or ethanol, does not conduct. A
strong electrolyte is fully ionised and conducts well; a weak electrolyte, such
as ethanoic acid or ammonia solution, is only slightly ionised and conducts
poorly.

The cathode is the electrode connected to the negative terminal, so cations
(positive ions) move to it and are discharged there by gaining electrons, which
is reduction. The anode is connected to the positive terminal, so anions move to
it and give up electrons there, which is oxidation. Reduction always happens at
the cathode.

## Preferential discharge

When more than one kind of ion could be discharged, three things decide which is:

1. Position in the electrochemical series. The lower an ion is in the discharge
   series, the more readily it is discharged. For cations the order of increasing
   ease of discharge is K+, Na+, Ca2+, Mg2+, Al3+, Zn2+, Fe2+, Pb2+, H+, Cu2+,
   Ag+, so Cu2+ is discharged in preference to H+, and H+ in preference to Na+.
   For anions the order of increasing ease of discharge is SO4 2-, NO3-, Cl-,
   Br-, I-, OH-, so OH- is discharged in preference to SO4 2-.
2. Concentration. A very concentrated solution of a halide will give the halogen
   at the anode even though OH- is normally discharged first: concentrated sodium
   chloride solution gives chlorine, while very dilute sodium chloride solution
   gives oxygen.
3. Nature of the electrode. Inert electrodes of platinum or carbon take no part
   in the reaction. An attackable electrode dissolves instead: with copper
   electrodes in copper(II) tetraoxosulphate(VI), the anode dissolves as
   Cu -> Cu2+ + 2e- while copper plates onto the cathode, and that is how copper
   is purified.

Electrolysing dilute tetraoxosulphate(VI) acid with inert electrodes gives
hydrogen at the cathode and oxygen at the anode in the volume ratio 2 to 1,
because 4 moles of electrons liberate 2 moles of hydrogen but only 1 mole of
oxygen.

## Faraday's laws and the calculation

Faraday's first law: the mass of a substance liberated at an electrode is
directly proportional to the quantity of electricity passed.

Faraday's second law: when the same quantity of electricity is passed through
different electrolytes, the masses liberated are proportional to their chemical
equivalents, that is to the molar mass divided by the charge on the ion.

The quantity of electricity is Q = It, with I in amperes and t in seconds, giving
Q in coulombs. One faraday is 96500 coulombs and is the charge on one mole of
electrons.

So one faraday deposits one mole of a singly charged ion, two faradays one mole
of a doubly charged ion, and so on.

Worked figures. One faraday, 96500 C, deposits 1 mole of silver from a silver
salt, because Ag+ + e- -> Ag needs one electron per atom; that is 108 g. The same
96500 C deposits only half a mole of copper from Cu2+, because
Cu2+ + 2e- -> Cu needs two electrons per atom; taking Cu = 64 that is 32 g. And
96500 C liberates half a mole of hydrogen gas, because 2H+ + 2e- -> H2; at s.t.p.
that is 0.5 x 22.4 = 11.2 dm3.

## Applications

Electrolysis is used to extract the reactive metals, which cannot be reduced by
carbon: sodium from molten sodium chloride in the Downs cell, and aluminium from
purified aluminium oxide dissolved in molten cryolite. It is used to purify
copper, with an impure anode and a pure cathode, the impurities falling below the
anode as anode sludge. It is used in electroplating, where the object to be
plated is made the cathode and the plating metal the anode, to resist corrosion
or to improve appearance. And the electrolysis of concentrated sodium chloride
solution gives chlorine, hydrogen and sodium hydroxide, which is the
chlor-alkali industry.
