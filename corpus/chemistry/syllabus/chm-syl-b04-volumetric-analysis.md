## What the syllabus requires

Describe how a standard solution is prepared and say what makes a substance a
primary standard. Describe the apparatus and procedure of an acid-alkali
titration and record results in the standard table. Choose a suitable indicator.
Calculate an unknown concentration from titration data using the balanced
equation, and convert the result to g/dm3.

## Standard solutions and apparatus

A standard solution is one whose concentration is known accurately. It is
prepared by weighing an accurate mass of a pure solid, dissolving it in a little
distilled water, transferring the solution and all the washings to a volumetric
flask, and making up to the mark.

A primary standard is a substance pure enough and stable enough to be weighed out
directly: it must be obtainable pure, must not absorb water or carbon dioxide
from the air, must not decompose on storage, and should have a reasonably high
molar mass so that weighing errors matter less. Anhydrous sodium
trioxocarbonate(IV) and ethanedioic acid crystals are used as primary standards.
Sodium hydroxide is not, because it absorbs both water and carbon dioxide from
the air, so its solutions must themselves be standardised against a primary
standard.

The pipette delivers a fixed volume, usually 20.0 or 25.0 cm3, and is rinsed with
the solution it is to deliver. The burette measures the variable volume added and
is also rinsed with the solution it will hold. The conical flask is rinsed with
distilled water only: rinsing it with the solution would add extra solute and
make the titre wrong.

Titrations are repeated until two or more titres agree within 0.10 cm3. Those are
the concordant titres, and only they are averaged. Volumes are read to the bottom
of the meniscus at eye level and recorded to two decimal places, the second being
0 or 5.

## Choosing an indicator

An indicator must change colour over the pH range in which the pH is changing
steeply at the end point.

- Strong acid with strong alkali, such as HCl with NaOH: methyl orange or
  phenolphthalein both work.
- Weak acid with strong alkali, such as ethanoic acid with NaOH: phenolphthalein,
  because the solution at the end point is alkaline.
- Strong acid with weak alkali, such as HCl with ammonia solution: methyl orange,
  because the solution at the end point is acidic.
- Weak acid with weak alkali: no indicator is suitable, because there is no sharp
  change in pH.

Methyl orange is red in acid and yellow in alkali, passing through orange.
Phenolphthalein is colourless in acid and pink in alkali.

## The calculation

Everything follows from the balanced equation. For an acid A reacting with a base
B in the mole ratio nA to nB,

(CA x VA) / (CB x VB) = nA / nB

where C is concentration in mol/dm3 and V is volume in cm3 or dm3, provided both
volumes are in the same unit.

Worked figure. 25.0 cm3 of sodium hydroxide solution is exactly neutralised by
20.0 cm3 of 0.100 mol/dm3 tetraoxosulphate(VI) acid. The equation is

H2SO4 + 2NaOH -> Na2SO4 + 2H2O

Moles of acid = 0.100 x 20.0/1000 = 0.00200 mol. The ratio is 1 acid to 2 alkali,
so moles of NaOH = 2 x 0.00200 = 0.00400 mol. That is in 25.0/1000 = 0.0250 dm3,
so the concentration is 0.00400/0.0250 = 0.16 mol/dm3. In g/dm3, with the molar
mass of NaOH equal to 23 + 16 + 1 = 40 g/mol, that is 0.16 x 40 = 6.4 g/dm3.

The most common mistake is to forget the mole ratio. Check the equation before
dividing.

## Not on this syllabus

Redox titrations treated as a full method with their own indicators, complexometric
titration with EDTA, back-titration as a standard technique, pH curve sketching
with calculated pH values at chosen points, buffer calculations, and conductimetric
or potentiometric end-point determination are not examined here.
