## What the syllabus requires

Give the general formula, the functional group and the naming of the alkanols,
the alkanoic acids and the alkanoates. Describe the preparation of ethanol by
the fermentation of sugar and by the hydration of ethene. Describe the
properties and uses of ethanol and of ethanoic acid, and state how the two are
distinguished. Describe esterification and the hydrolysis of an alkanoate,
including the alkaline hydrolysis of fats and oils to soap. Compare soap with
soapless detergents and explain how each cleans. Find a molecular formula from a
relative molecular mass, and calculate a percentage composition.

## The three functional groups

| Series | Functional group | General formula | First members |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Alkanols | -OH, hydroxyl | CnH2n+1OH | Methanol CH3OH, ethanol C2H5OH |
| Alkanoic acids | -COOH, carboxyl | CnH2n+1COOH | Methanoic acid HCOOH, ethanoic acid CH3COOH |
| Alkanoates | -COO-, ester link | RCOOR' | Methyl methanoate HCOOCH3, ethyl ethanoate CH3COOC2H5 |

An alkanol is named from the alkane of the same carbon number, with the ending
-ol: methanol, ethanol, propanol, butanol. An alkanoic acid takes the ending
-oic acid, and the carbon of the -COOH group is counted as part of the chain, so
ethanoic acid, CH3COOH, has two carbon atoms in all.

Alkanols are classed by how many carbon atoms are attached to the carbon
carrying the -OH group: primary if one or none, secondary if two, tertiary if
three. Propan-1-ol is primary and propan-2-ol is secondary, and they are
position isomers of each other with the formula C3H7OH.

An alkanoate is named in two parts. The first part comes from the alkanol and
the second from the acid, so ethanol with ethanoic acid gives ethyl ethanoate,
and methanol with ethanoic acid gives methyl ethanoate. The alkanol part is
written first but supplies the atoms on the right of the ester link.

Worked figure. An alkanol has a relative molecular mass of 46. An alkanol is
CnH2n+1OH, which is CnH2n+2O, so its mass is 12n + (2n + 2) + 16 = 14n + 18.
Then 14n + 18 = 46 gives 14n = 28 and n = 2, so the formula is C2H5OH, ethanol.
The same arithmetic for an alkanoic acid, CnH2nO2, uses 14n + 32: a relative
molecular mass of 60 gives 14n = 28, so n = 2 and the acid is CH3COOH.

## Alkanols

Ethanol is made in two ways. The first is fermentation. Yeast supplies the
enzyme zymase, which converts glucose to ethanol in the absence of air at about
25 to 35 degrees Celsius:

C6H12O6 -> 2C2H5OH + 2CO2

Fermentation stops of its own accord at roughly 12 to 14 per cent ethanol,
because the alcohol kills the yeast, and the mixture is then concentrated by
fractional distillation. The second way is industrial: ethene from the cracking
of petroleum is hydrated with steam over a tetraoxophosphate(V) acid catalyst,
H3PO4, at high temperature and pressure.

Ethanol is a colourless liquid with a characteristic smell, neutral to litmus,
and miscible with water in all proportions because of hydrogen bonding. Its
reactions worth knowing are these.

It burns completely in plenty of air with a pale blue flame:

C2H5OH + 3O2 -> 2CO2 + 3H2O

It reacts with sodium, more slowly than water does, giving hydrogen and sodium
ethoxide:

2C2H5OH + 2Na -> 2C2H5ONa + H2

It is oxidised by warming with acidified potassium tetraoxomanganate(VII), which
goes from purple to colourless, or with acidified potassium
heptaoxodichromate(VI), which goes from orange to green. A primary alkanol is
oxidised first to the alkanal and then to the alkanoic acid, so ethanol gives
ethanoic acid. It is dehydrated to ethene by excess concentrated
tetraoxosulphate(VI) acid at about 170 degrees Celsius, or by passing the vapour
over heated aluminium oxide. And it reacts with an alkanoic acid to give an
alkanoate, which is the next section.

Ethanol is used as a solvent in paints, perfumes and drugs, as a fuel and as a
component of alcoholic drinks. Methanol is poisonous and causes blindness;
industrial spirit is deliberately made undrinkable by adding it.

## Alkanoic acids

Ethanoic acid is the familiar member, the acid in vinegar. Alkanoic acids are
weak acids: they ionise only partially in water, so a solution of ethanoic acid
of a given concentration has a higher pH and conducts less well than
hydrochloric acid of the same concentration.

CH3COOH reversible CH3COO- + H+

Being acids, they show the usual reactions. They turn blue litmus red. They
neutralise alkalis to give a salt and water, sodium hydroxide giving sodium
ethanoate. They react with reactive metals to give hydrogen. And they give
carbon(IV) oxide with a trioxocarbonate(IV) or a hydrogentrioxocarbonate(IV):

2CH3COOH + Na2CO3 -> 2CH3COONa + H2O + CO2

That last reaction is the test that distinguishes an alkanoic acid from an
alkanol. Both give hydrogen with sodium, so sodium proves nothing; but only the
acid turns blue litmus red and only the acid gives effervescence with sodium
trioxocarbonate(IV), and the gas turns lime water milky.

## Alkanoates and esterification

An alkanol warmed with an alkanoic acid and a little concentrated
tetraoxosulphate(VI) acid gives an alkanoate and water. This is esterification:

CH3COOH + C2H5OH reversible CH3COOC2H5 + H2O

The concentrated acid is a catalyst and a dehydrating agent. The reaction is
reversible and does not go to completion, so the yield is raised by using an
excess of one reactant or by removing the water. Alkanoates are neutral liquids
with sweet, fruity smells, and they are used as flavourings, as perfumes and as
solvents for paints and lacquers.

Running the reaction backwards is hydrolysis. With dilute acid it is reversible
and gives back the alkanol and the alkanoic acid. With hot sodium hydroxide
solution it is not reversible, because the acid is converted to its salt, and it
gives the alkanol and the sodium salt of the acid. That alkaline hydrolysis is
called saponification.

Fats and oils are naturally occurring alkanoates: esters of propane-1,2,3-triol,
which is glycerol, with long-chain alkanoic acids. Fats are solid and largely
saturated; oils are liquid and largely unsaturated, which is why an oil
decolourises bromine water and why hydrogen with a nickel catalyst hardens a
vegetable oil into margarine.

## Soaps and detergents

Soap is made by boiling a fat or an oil with concentrated sodium hydroxide
solution. The ester links are hydrolysed, giving glycerol and the sodium salts
of the long-chain acids, which are the soap. Common salt is then stirred in to
lower the solubility of the soap so that it separates as a solid, a step called
salting out. Potassium hydroxide gives a softer, liquid soap.

A soap ion has two ends that behave differently. The long hydrocarbon tail is
non-polar and dissolves in grease; the -COO- head is ionic and dissolves in
water. So a soap lowers the surface tension of the water, lets it wet the cloth,
and pulls the grease off into droplets each surrounded by soap ions, an emulsion
that rinses away.

Soapless detergents are made from long-chain hydrocarbons or alkanols treated
with concentrated tetraoxosulphate(VI) acid and then neutralised with sodium
hydroxide, giving sodium alkylbenzenesulphonates and related compounds. They
clean by exactly the same mechanism. Their advantage is that their calcium and
magnesium salts are soluble, so they lather in hard water instead of forming
scum. Their disadvantage is that the branched-chain kinds are not readily broken
down by bacteria, so they persist and foam on rivers.

## Not on this syllabus

Reaction mechanisms in detail are not examined here: nucleophilic substitution
of the hydroxyl group with curly arrows, the addition-elimination mechanism of
esterification with its tetrahedral intermediate, named intermediates and
carbocation stability, and the proton-transfer steps of acid catalysis. Also
outside this syllabus are optical activity and chirality, the quantitative
treatment of esterification by an equilibrium constant or a rate equation,
infrared and nuclear magnetic resonance spectra for identifying a functional
group, Grignard and other organometallic reagents, and the industrial plant
design and enzyme kinetics of fermentation.
