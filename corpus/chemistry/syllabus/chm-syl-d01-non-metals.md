## What the syllabus requires

Describe the laboratory preparation, physical properties, chemical properties,
tests and uses of hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen and its compounds, carbon and its
oxides, sulphur and its compounds, and chlorine and hydrogen chloride. Write
balanced equations for the reactions named. Use the stoichiometry of those
equations in calculations.

## Hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen

Hydrogen is prepared in the laboratory by the action of a dilute acid on a
moderately reactive metal:

Zn + H2SO4 -> ZnSO4 + H2

It is the least dense gas known, is neutral to litmus, and burns with a pale blue
flame. The test is that it burns with a pop when a lighted splint is applied. It
is a good reducing agent, reducing heated copper(II) oxide to copper. It is used
in the Haber process and in hardening vegetable oils to margarine.

Oxygen is prepared by the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide with manganese(IV)
oxide as catalyst:

2H2O2 -> 2H2O + O2

It is neutral to litmus, slightly soluble in water, and relights a glowing
splint, which is its test. It supports combustion without burning itself and
forms oxides with metals and non-metals. It is used in welding, in steel making
and in hospitals.

Nitrogen is obtained industrially by the fractional distillation of liquefied
air. It is very unreactive because of the strong triple bond in N2, which is why
it is used as an inert atmosphere and why nitrogen fixation needs the high
temperature and pressure of the Haber process.

Ammonia is prepared in the laboratory by warming an ammonium salt with an alkali:

2NH4Cl + Ca(OH)2 -> CaCl2 + 2H2O + 2NH3

It is the only common alkaline gas, so it turns damp red litmus blue, and it
gives dense white fumes of ammonium chloride with a rod dipped in concentrated
hydrochloric acid. Those are its two tests. It is extremely soluble in water,
shown by the fountain experiment, and it is dried with quicklime rather than with
concentrated tetraoxosulphate(VI) acid, which would react with it.

Trioxonitrate(V) acid, HNO3, is made industrially by the catalytic oxidation of
ammonia in the Ostwald process. Dilute, it behaves as a typical strong acid but
usually gives oxides of nitrogen rather than hydrogen with metals, because it is
also an oxidising agent. Concentrated and hot, it is a powerful oxidising agent;
cold and concentrated it renders iron and aluminium passive by forming an oxide
layer.

## Carbon and its oxides

Carbon occurs as diamond, graphite and the amorphous forms such as charcoal and
coke, which are allotropes: different forms of the same element in the same
physical state.

Carbon(IV) oxide, CO2, is prepared by the action of dilute hydrochloric acid on a
trioxocarbonate(IV):

CaCO3 + 2HCl -> CaCl2 + H2O + CO2

It is denser than air, slightly soluble giving a weakly acidic solution, and does
not support combustion. The test is that it turns lime water milky; with excess
carbon dioxide the milkiness clears as the soluble hydrogentrioxocarbonate(IV)
forms. It is used in fire extinguishers, in drinks, and as solid carbon dioxide
for refrigeration.

Carbon(II) oxide, CO, is a neutral oxide, burns with a blue flame, and is a
strong reducing agent used in the blast furnace. It is extremely poisonous
because it combines with haemoglobin far more strongly than oxygen does, and it
is produced whenever carbon burns in a limited supply of air.

Worked figure. Heating 25 g of calcium trioxocarbonate(IV) gives, from
CaCO3 -> CaO + CO2, an amount of 25/100 = 0.25 mol of carbon dioxide, which at
s.t.p. occupies 0.25 x 22.4 = 5.6 dm3.

## Sulphur and chlorine

Sulphur has two crystalline allotropes: rhombic sulphur, stable below
96 degrees Celsius, and monoclinic sulphur, stable above it up to the melting
point.

Sulphur(IV) oxide, SO2, is prepared by the action of a dilute acid on a
trioxosulphate(IV), or by burning sulphur in air. It has a choking smell, turns
damp blue litmus red, and bleaches damp coloured materials by reduction, a
bleaching that is not permanent because the colour returns on exposure to air. It
turns acidified potassium heptaoxodichromate(VI) from orange to green, which is
its test.

Tetraoxosulphate(VI) acid is made by the Contact process. Dilute, it is a typical
strong dibasic acid. Concentrated, it is a dehydrating agent, charring sugar and
removing the elements of water from hydrated salts, and it is an oxidising agent
when hot.

Chlorine is prepared by oxidising concentrated hydrochloric acid with
manganese(IV) oxide, or by the action of the acid on potassium
tetraoxomanganate(VII). It is a greenish-yellow poisonous gas, denser than air,
and it bleaches damp litmus permanently by oxidation. It displaces bromine and
iodine from their salts. It is used to sterilise water and to make bleaching
powder and hydrochloric acid.

Hydrogen chloride is prepared by the action of concentrated tetraoxosulphate(VI)
acid on sodium chloride. It fumes in moist air, is extremely soluble in water
giving hydrochloric acid, and gives dense white fumes with ammonia.

## Not on this syllabus

Detailed industrial plant design, the full mechanism of the Ostwald or Contact
process step by step with intermediates, the chemistry of the noble gases beyond
their inertness, interhalogen compounds, the oxoacids of chlorine treated
systematically, and spectroscopic identification of these gases are not examined
here.
