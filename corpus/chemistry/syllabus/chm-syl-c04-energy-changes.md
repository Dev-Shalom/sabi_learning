## What the syllabus requires

Distinguish exothermic from endothermic changes and give the sign of the enthalpy
change for each. Define the standard enthalpy changes of reaction, formation,
combustion, neutralisation and solution. Draw and interpret an energy profile
diagram showing activation energy. Calculate an enthalpy change from a
temperature rise using Q = mc x change in temperature.

## Exothermic and endothermic

An exothermic change releases heat to the surroundings, so the surroundings get
warmer and the enthalpy change, written as the heat of reaction, is negative.
Burning fuels, neutralisation, respiration, the dissolving of concentrated
tetraoxosulphate(VI) acid in water, and the setting of cement are exothermic.

An endothermic change absorbs heat from the surroundings, so the surroundings get
cooler and the enthalpy change is positive. Photosynthesis, the thermal
decomposition of trioxocarbonates(IV), the dissolving of ammonium
trioxonitrate(V) in water, and melting and boiling are endothermic.

The sign convention is from the point of view of the reacting system. A negative
heat of reaction means the products hold less energy than the reactants, and the
difference has gone out as heat.

## The named enthalpy changes

- Heat of reaction: the heat change when the molar amounts shown in the equation
  react under stated conditions.
- Heat of formation: the heat change when one mole of a compound is formed from
  its elements in their standard states. The heat of formation of an element in
  its standard state is zero by definition.
- Heat of combustion: the heat change when one mole of a substance is completely
  burnt in oxygen. It is always negative.
- Heat of neutralisation: the heat change when one mole of water is formed by the
  reaction of an acid with a base. For a strong acid with a strong alkali it is
  close to -57 kJ/mol whichever pair is used, because the reaction is always the
  same one, H+ + OH- -> H2O. It is less exothermic for a weak acid, because some
  of the energy released goes into ionising the remaining acid molecules.
- Heat of solution: the heat change when one mole of a substance is dissolved in
  so much water that further dilution produces no further heat change.

## Energy profiles and activation energy

An energy profile plots energy against the progress of the reaction. The
reactants sit at one level, the products at another, and between them is a hump.
The height of the hump above the reactants is the activation energy: the minimum
energy a colliding pair must have before they can react at all. The difference
between the reactant and product levels is the heat of reaction.

For an exothermic reaction the products lie below the reactants; for an
endothermic one they lie above. A catalyst lowers the hump by providing an
alternative route of lower activation energy, but it does not move the reactant
or product levels, so it does not change the heat of reaction.

A reaction can be exothermic and still need heating to start, because the
activation energy has to be supplied before the energy the reaction releases
becomes available. That is why a match must be struck.

## Calculating from a temperature change

The heat gained or lost by the water in a calorimeter is

Q = m x c x change in temperature

with m the mass of water in grams, c the specific heat capacity of water,
4.2 J per gram per kelvin, and the temperature change in degrees Celsius, which
is the same size as a change in kelvin.

The heat of reaction is then the heat divided by the number of moles that
reacted, with a negative sign if the temperature rose.

Worked figure. Burning a fuel raises the temperature of 100 g of water by
5 degrees Celsius, and 0.02 mol of the fuel was burnt. The heat given out is
Q = 100 x 4.2 x 5 = 2100 J. Per mole that is 2100/0.02 = 105000 J/mol, which is
105 kJ/mol, and since the temperature rose the heat of combustion is written as
-105 kJ/mol.

Values measured this way come out low, because heat is lost to the air and to the
apparatus and some fuel may burn incompletely.

## Not on this syllabus

Entropy and Gibbs free energy and any calculation of the feasibility of a
reaction from them, bond-enthalpy calculations of reaction enthalpy, Hess's law
cycles requiring the construction of a full enthalpy cycle, Born-Haber cycles and
lattice energies, and calorimetry corrections using bomb calorimeter constants
are not examined here.
