## What the syllabus requires

Define the rate of a chemical reaction and state its units. Describe how a rate
is measured. State the factors that affect a rate and explain each in terms of
collision theory. Define a catalyst and distinguish the kinds. Calculate an
average rate from a change in mass, volume or concentration over a measured time.

## What a rate is, and how it is measured

The rate of a reaction is the change in the amount of a reactant or product per
unit time. Depending on what is measured, the unit is mol/dm3 per second, cm3 per
second, or grams per second.

A reaction that gives off a gas can be followed by collecting the gas in a
syringe or over water and recording the volume at intervals, or by standing the
flask on a balance and recording the falling mass. A reaction that produces a
precipitate can be followed by timing how long a cross drawn under the flask
takes to disappear. A reaction involving a coloured species can be followed by
the change in colour intensity, and one involving an acid by withdrawing samples
and titrating them.

The rate is greatest at the start, when the reactants are most concentrated, and
falls steadily to zero as they are used up. On a graph of product against time
this shows as a curve that is steepest at the origin and flattens to a horizontal
line. The average rate over an interval is the total change divided by the total
time; the instantaneous rate at a point is the gradient of the tangent there.

Worked figure. A reaction gives off 48 cm3 of gas in the first 30 s. The average
rate over that interval is 48/30 = 1.6 cm3 per second. If in a different
experiment the concentration of a reactant falls from 0.80 mol/dm3 to
0.50 mol/dm3 in 50 s, the average rate is (0.80 - 0.50)/50 = 0.30/50 =
0.006 mol/dm3 per second.

## Collision theory and the factors

For a reaction to happen, particles must collide, with enough energy to overcome
the activation energy, and in the right orientation. Anything that increases the
frequency of such effective collisions increases the rate.

- Concentration. A more concentrated solution has more particles in the same
  volume, so collisions are more frequent and the rate is higher.
- Pressure, for reactions between gases. Raising the pressure squeezes the same
  number of particles into a smaller volume, which is the same thing as raising
  the concentration.
- Surface area. A solid reacts only at its surface, so powdering it or using
  smaller lumps exposes more surface and speeds the reaction up. This is why a
  flour or coal dust can explode while the bulk solid merely burns.
- Temperature. Raising the temperature makes the particles move faster, so they
  collide more often, and, far more importantly, it greatly increases the
  proportion of particles with energy above the activation energy. As a rough
  guide a rise of 10 degrees Celsius roughly doubles the rate of many reactions.
- Catalyst. A catalyst provides an alternative route of lower activation energy,
  so a larger fraction of collisions is effective.
- Light, for photochemical reactions such as the decomposition of silver halides
  in photography and the reaction of methane with chlorine.

## Catalysts

A catalyst is a substance that alters the rate of a chemical reaction while
remaining chemically unchanged in mass and composition at the end. A positive
catalyst speeds a reaction up; a negative catalyst or inhibitor slows it down,
which is what a food preservative does.

In homogeneous catalysis the catalyst is in the same phase as the reactants; in
heterogeneous catalysis it is in a different phase, usually a solid catalysing a
gas reaction. Enzymes are biological catalysts, highly specific, and destroyed
outside a narrow range of temperature and pH.

Familiar examples: manganese(IV) oxide for the decomposition of hydrogen
peroxide, iron in the Haber process, vanadium(V) oxide in the Contact process,
and nickel in the hardening of vegetable oils.

A catalyst does not change the position of equilibrium or the heat of reaction.
It only lets the system get where it was going sooner.

## Not on this syllabus

Rate equations, the order of a reaction and the determination of order from
initial-rate or half-life data, the rate constant and its units, the Arrhenius
equation and activation energies calculated from it, reaction mechanisms with
rate-determining steps identified, and the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution treated
mathematically are not examined here.
