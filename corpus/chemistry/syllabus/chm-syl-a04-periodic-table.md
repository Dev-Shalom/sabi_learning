## What the syllabus requires

State the modern periodic law and explain how the table is arranged. Deduce the
group and period of an element from its electron configuration. Describe and
explain the trends across a period and down a group in atomic radius, ionisation
energy, electronegativity, metallic character and the acid-base character of the
oxides. Give the characteristic properties of Groups I, II, VII and 0.

## The law and the arrangement

The modern periodic law states that the properties of the elements are a periodic
function of their atomic numbers. The elements are arranged in order of
increasing atomic number in horizontal periods and vertical groups.

A group is a vertical column of elements with the same number of outer-shell
electrons, and therefore similar chemical properties. A period is a horizontal
row in which the outer shell is being filled, so properties change steadily from
left to right.

An element with Z = 17 has the configuration 2, 8, 7. Seven outer electrons puts
it in Group VII, and three occupied shells puts it in Period 3, so it is
chlorine. An element with Z = 12 has the configuration 2, 8, 2, so it is in Group
II, Period 3, and it is magnesium.

## Trends across Period 3

Across the period from sodium to argon the nuclear charge increases by one each
time while electrons enter the same shell. The increasing pull of the nucleus on
the same shell means:

- Atomic radius decreases. Sodium is the largest atom in the period, chlorine one
  of the smallest.
- Ionisation energy increases, because the outer electron is held more tightly.
- Electronegativity increases, because the atom attracts a bonding pair more
  strongly. Sodium is among the least electronegative elements, chlorine among
  the most.
- Metallic character decreases and non-metallic character increases. Sodium,
  magnesium and aluminium are metals; silicon is a metalloid; phosphorus, sulphur
  and chlorine are non-metals; argon is a noble gas.
- The oxides change from basic to acidic. Na2O and MgO are basic and dissolve in
  water to give alkaline solutions; Al2O3 is amphoteric, reacting with both acids
  and alkalis; SiO2, P4O10, SO2, SO3 and Cl2O7 are acidic.

## Trends down a group

Going down a group a new shell is added at each step, so:

- Atomic radius increases.
- Ionisation energy decreases, because the outer electron is further from the
  nucleus and shielded by more inner shells.
- Electronegativity decreases.
- Metallic character increases, which is why in Group IV carbon is a non-metal,
  silicon and germanium are metalloids, and tin and lead are metals.

In Group I, reactivity increases down the group because the outer electron is
more easily lost. In Group VII, reactivity decreases down the group because an
electron is less easily gained: fluorine is the most reactive halogen and iodine
the least. A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive one from its salt
solution, so chlorine displaces bromine from potassium bromide solution.

## The characteristic groups

Group I, the alkali metals, have one outer electron, form ions of charge +1, are
soft and low-melting, react vigorously with water to give an alkali and hydrogen,
and are stored under oil.

Group II, the alkaline earth metals, have two outer electrons and form ions of
charge +2. They are harder and higher-melting than Group I and react with water
less vigorously; magnesium reacts readily with steam rather than with cold water.

Group VII, the halogens, have seven outer electrons, form ions of charge -1, and
exist as diatomic molecules. They are coloured, and the colour deepens down the
group: chlorine is a greenish-yellow gas, bromine a red-brown liquid and iodine a
grey-black solid that sublimes to a purple vapour.

Group 0, the noble gases, have full outer shells, are monatomic, and are
chemically very unreactive. They are used where an inert atmosphere is wanted:
helium in balloons, argon in light bulbs and in welding, neon in advertising
signs.

## Not on this syllabus

Quantitative electronegativity scales and their use to predict percentage ionic
character, detailed chemistry of the transition elements beyond naming them and
noting variable oxidation state and coloured ions, the lanthanides and actinides,
the anomalous first-member behaviour of each group treated as a theory, and the
diagonal relationship as an examinable principle are not examined here.
