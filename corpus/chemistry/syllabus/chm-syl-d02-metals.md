## What the syllabus requires

State the general physical and chemical properties of metals and relate them to
metallic bonding. Give the activity series and use it to predict displacement
reactions and the method of extraction. Describe the extraction of sodium,
aluminium and iron, and the manufacture of steel. Describe the important
compounds of sodium, calcium and iron, and the tests for their ions. Explain
corrosion and its prevention. Calculate the percentage of a metal in an ore.

## General properties and the activity series

Metals are lustrous, malleable, ductile, good conductors of heat and electricity,
and usually dense with high melting points, all of which follow from a lattice of
positive ions in a sea of delocalised electrons. Chemically they lose electrons
to form positive ions, they form basic oxides, and they displace hydrogen from
acids if they are reactive enough.

The activity series in decreasing order of reactivity is potassium, sodium,
calcium, magnesium, aluminium, zinc, iron, lead, hydrogen, copper, mercury,
silver, gold.

A metal higher in the series displaces one lower down from its salt solution: an
iron nail put into copper(II) tetraoxosulphate(VI) solution becomes coated with
copper and the blue colour fades. Metals above hydrogen displace hydrogen from
dilute acids; copper, silver and gold do not. Potassium, sodium and calcium react
with cold water; magnesium reacts readily with steam; iron reacts with steam
reversibly when red hot; copper does not react with water at all.

The position in the series also decides the method of extraction. The very
reactive metals at the top must be got by electrolysis of the molten compound.
The middle of the series can be reduced with carbon or carbon(II) oxide. The
unreactive metals at the bottom occur native or are obtained by simply roasting
their ores.

## Extraction

Sodium is extracted by the electrolysis of molten sodium chloride in the Downs
cell, with calcium chloride added to lower the melting point. Sodium is formed at
the steel cathode and chlorine at the graphite anode.

Aluminium is extracted by the electrolysis of purified aluminium oxide, obtained
from bauxite, dissolved in molten cryolite to lower the working temperature. The
aluminium collects at the carbon cathode, and oxygen at the carbon anodes
gradually burns them away, so they must be replaced.

Iron is extracted in the blast furnace from haematite, Fe2O3, with coke and
limestone. Coke burns to carbon(IV) oxide, which is reduced by more hot coke to
carbon(II) oxide, and that is the actual reducing agent:

Fe2O3 + 3CO -> 2Fe + 3CO2

The limestone decomposes to calcium oxide, which combines with the sandy
impurities to form a slag of calcium trioxosilicate(IV) that floats on the molten
iron and is run off separately. The product is pig iron, containing about 4 per
cent carbon, which is brittle. Steel is made by blowing oxygen through the molten
iron to burn off most of the carbon, then adding the wanted amount back together
with other elements: chromium and nickel give stainless steel, manganese gives
hardness.

Worked figure. The percentage of iron in pure haematite, Fe2O3, is found from the
formula mass. With Fe = 56 and O = 16, the formula mass is
(2 x 56) + (3 x 16) = 112 + 48 = 160. The iron accounts for 112 of that, so the
percentage of iron is (112/160) x 100 = 70 per cent.

## Important compounds and the tests for their ions

Sodium hydroxide is made by the electrolysis of brine and is used in soap making
and in paper. Sodium trioxocarbonate(IV), washing soda, is made by the Solvay
process and is used to soften water and in glass making. Sodium
hydrogentrioxocarbonate(IV), baking soda, decomposes on heating to give carbon
dioxide, which is what makes bread rise.

Calcium trioxocarbonate(IV) occurs as limestone, marble and chalk. Heated
strongly it gives calcium oxide, quicklime; quicklime with water gives calcium
hydroxide, slaked lime, whose solution is lime water.

Iron forms two series of salts. Iron(II) salts are pale green in solution and are
reducing agents, being readily oxidised to iron(III). Iron(III) salts are yellow
or brown in solution.

The tests worth knowing use sodium hydroxide solution added to a solution of the
salt. A white precipitate soluble in excess indicates aluminium, zinc or lead. A
white precipitate insoluble in excess indicates calcium or magnesium. A dirty
green precipitate indicates iron(II); a reddish-brown precipitate indicates
iron(III); a pale blue precipitate that dissolves in excess ammonia to a deep
blue solution indicates copper(II). A flame test gives a golden-yellow flame for
sodium, lilac for potassium and brick-red for calcium.

## Corrosion

Rusting is the corrosion of iron, and it needs both air and water: iron kept in
dry air or in boiled water under oil does not rust. Rust is hydrated iron(III)
oxide. Salt and acid speed it up.

It is prevented by keeping air and water away, with paint, grease, plastic
coating or a layer of another metal; by galvanising, in which the iron is coated
with zinc, which also protects it sacrificially if the coating is scratched,
because zinc is above iron in the series; by cathodic protection using blocks of
magnesium or zinc attached to a ship or a pipeline; and by alloying, as in
stainless steel.

Aluminium appears not to corrode although it is high in the activity series. It
does react, but the thin oxide layer it forms sticks firmly to the surface and
protects the metal underneath, which is why aluminium is used for window frames
and cooking pots.

## Not on this syllabus

Transition-metal complex ion theory including ligands, co-ordination number,
isomerism of complexes and the explanation of their colour by crystal-field
splitting; the full thermodynamics of extraction by Ellingham diagrams;
quantitative treatment of sacrificial protection by electrode potentials; and the
detailed metallurgy of alloys by phase diagram are not examined here.
