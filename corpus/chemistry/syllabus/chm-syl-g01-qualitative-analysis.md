## What the syllabus requires

Carry out and record simple tests on an unknown substance, writing what was
done, what was seen and what it shows. Identify the common gases by their
properties. Identify the cations calcium, magnesium, aluminium, zinc, lead,
iron(II), iron(III), copper(II) and ammonium by the action of sodium hydroxide
solution and of aqueous ammonia, added first a little and then in excess.
Identify the anions trioxocarbonate(IV), tetraoxosulphate(VI),
trioxosulphate(IV), chloride and trioxonitrate(V). Carry out a flame test. The
same material is examined in the Alternative to Practical Work paper, where the
observations are supplied and you are asked for the inferences.

## How the tests are recorded

The answer is written in three columns: the test, the observation and the
inference. The test says what was added to what. The observation says only what
was seen, heard or smelt, never what it was. The inference names what that
observation shows, and it should name an ion rather than a compound unless the
evidence justifies more.

Two habits earn marks. Add a reagent a little at a time and then in excess, and
say what happened at each stage, because for several ions the whole
identification lies in what excess does. And keep the observation honest: a
white precipitate is a white precipitate, and calling it barium
tetraoxosulphate(VI) before the test for it is guessing.

Warm gently, hold the tube pointing away from yourself and others, use a small
portion of the sample so that a test can be repeated, and never taste anything.

## Tests for gases

| Gas | Test and observation |
| --- | --- |
| Hydrogen, H2 | Burns with a pop when a lighted splint is applied |
| Oxygen, O2 | Relights a glowing splint |
| Carbon(IV) oxide, CO2 | Turns lime water milky; the milkiness clears in excess gas |
| Sulphur(IV) oxide, SO2 | Choking smell; turns damp blue litmus red; turns acidified potassium heptaoxodichromate(VI) from orange to green |
| Ammonia, NH3 | Turns damp red litmus blue; dense white fumes with a rod dipped in concentrated hydrochloric acid |
| Chlorine, Cl2 | Greenish-yellow, choking; bleaches damp litmus permanently; turns damp starch-iodide paper blue-black |
| Hydrogen chloride, HCl | Fumes in moist air; turns damp blue litmus red; white fumes with ammonia, but no bleaching |
| Nitrogen(IV) oxide, NO2 | Reddish-brown, choking; turns damp blue litmus red |
| Hydrogen sulphide, H2S | Smell of rotten eggs; blackens paper moistened with lead(II) ethanoate |
| Water vapour | Turns white anhydrous copper(II) tetraoxosulphate(VI) blue, or blue cobalt(II) chloride paper pink |

Ammonia is the only common alkaline gas, so damp red litmus turning blue is
enough on its own. Sulphur(IV) oxide and chlorine both bleach, but the
sulphur(IV) oxide bleaching fades on standing in air and the chlorine bleaching
does not.

## Tests for cations with sodium hydroxide solution

Add sodium hydroxide solution to the solution of the salt drop by drop, then in
excess.

| Ion | With a little NaOH | With excess NaOH |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ca2+ | White precipitate | Insoluble; the precipitate remains |
| Mg2+ | White precipitate | Insoluble; the precipitate remains |
| Al3+ | White precipitate | Dissolves to a colourless solution |
| Zn2+ | White precipitate | Dissolves to a colourless solution |
| Pb2+ | White precipitate | Dissolves to a colourless solution |
| Fe2+ | Dirty green precipitate | Insoluble; browns slowly in air |
| Fe3+ | Reddish-brown precipitate | Insoluble |
| Cu2+ | Pale blue precipitate | Insoluble |
| NH4+ | No precipitate | Ammonia given off on warming |

The three that dissolve in excess sodium hydroxide are aluminium, zinc and lead,
because their hydroxides are amphoteric and react with the excess alkali to give
a soluble salt. That behaviour is what separates them from calcium and
magnesium, whose precipitates are also white but stay put. Calcium gives a white
precipitate only from a solution that is not too dilute, since calcium hydroxide
is sparingly soluble rather than insoluble.

The ammonium test is different in kind: there is no precipitate at all, and the
identification is the ammonia given off when the mixture is warmed, detected by
its smell and by damp red litmus turning blue.

## Tests for cations with aqueous ammonia

Now repeat with aqueous ammonia, again a little and then in excess. Comparing
the two sets is how the white precipitates are told apart.

| Ion | With a little NH3 solution | With excess NH3 solution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ca2+ | No precipitate | No precipitate |
| Mg2+ | White precipitate | Insoluble |
| Al3+ | White precipitate | Insoluble |
| Zn2+ | White precipitate | Dissolves to a colourless solution |
| Pb2+ | White precipitate | Insoluble |
| Fe2+ | Dirty green precipitate | Insoluble |
| Fe3+ | Reddish-brown precipitate | Insoluble |
| Cu2+ | Pale blue precipitate | Dissolves to a deep blue solution |

The two that dissolve in excess aqueous ammonia are zinc and copper(II), and
they are unmistakable from each other because the copper solution is deep blue
and the zinc one is colourless.

Put the two tables side by side and each white precipitate is settled.
Aluminium dissolves in excess sodium hydroxide but not in excess ammonia. Zinc
dissolves in both. Lead dissolves in excess sodium hydroxide but not in excess
ammonia, and is confirmed separately: dilute hydrochloric acid gives a white
precipitate of lead(II) chloride that dissolves in hot water, and potassium
iodide solution gives a yellow precipitate. Magnesium dissolves in neither.
Calcium dissolves in neither and gives no precipitate with ammonia at all. And
copper(II) is the one whose pale blue precipitate dissolves in excess ammonia
but not in excess sodium hydroxide.

## Flame tests

Clean a platinum or nichrome wire by dipping it in concentrated hydrochloric
acid and holding it in the flame until it colours the flame no longer. Moisten
it with the acid again, pick up a little of the solid, and hold it at the edge
of a hot non-luminous Bunsen flame.

| Metal ion | Flame colour |
| --- | --- |
| Sodium | Golden-yellow |
| Potassium | Lilac |
| Calcium | Brick-red |
| Copper(II) | Green to blue-green |
| Barium | Apple-green |
| Lithium | Crimson |

Sodium is present as a trace almost everywhere and its yellow is strong enough
to mask the lilac of potassium, so a brief yellow flash is not evidence of
sodium in the sample.

## Tests for anions

For a trioxocarbonate(IV), CO3 2-, or a hydrogentrioxocarbonate(IV), add dilute
hydrochloric acid to the solid or its solution. There is effervescence, and the
gas turns lime water milky, which identifies carbon(IV) oxide:

CaCO3 + 2HCl -> CaCl2 + H2O + CO2

For a tetraoxosulphate(VI), SO4 2-, acidify the solution with dilute
hydrochloric acid, then add barium chloride solution. A white precipitate of
barium tetraoxosulphate(VI) that does not dissolve in the acid confirms it. The
acid must go in first: it destroys a trioxocarbonate(IV) or a
trioxosulphate(IV), either of which would otherwise give a white precipitate
too.

For a trioxosulphate(IV), SO3 2-, add dilute hydrochloric acid and warm. Gas
with a choking smell is given off which turns acidified potassium
heptaoxodichromate(VI) from orange to green. With barium chloride it gives a
white precipitate, but unlike the tetraoxosulphate(VI) that precipitate
dissolves in dilute hydrochloric acid with effervescence.

For a chloride, Cl-, acidify with dilute trioxonitrate(V) acid, then add silver
trioxonitrate(V) solution. A white precipitate of silver chloride forms which
dissolves in aqueous ammonia and darkens in sunlight. Warming the solid with
concentrated tetraoxosulphate(VI) acid gives steamy fumes of hydrogen chloride
that give white fumes with ammonia.

For a trioxonitrate(V), NO3-, use the brown ring test. Add freshly prepared
iron(II) tetraoxosulphate(VI) solution to the solution under test, then pour
concentrated tetraoxosulphate(VI) acid slowly down the side of the tilted tube
so that it forms a layer underneath. A brown ring at the junction of the two
layers confirms a trioxonitrate(V). Warming the solid with copper turnings and
concentrated tetraoxosulphate(VI) acid gives reddish-brown fumes of
nitrogen(IV) oxide.

## Not on this syllabus

Instrumental analysis is not examined here: atomic absorption spectroscopy,
flame photometry, paper, thin-layer, gas and high-performance liquid
chromatography, mass spectrometry, infrared and nuclear magnetic resonance
spectra, ion-selective electrodes and X-ray methods. Nor is the full systematic
separation of cations into analytical groups by hydrogen sulphide, the
solubility-product arithmetic of why a precipitate appears or redissolves, the
formulae and systematic names of the complex ions formed when a precipitate
dissolves in excess reagent, or quantitative gravimetric analysis by weighing a
precipitate to constant mass.
