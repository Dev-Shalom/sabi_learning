## What the syllabus requires

Define oxidation and reduction in terms of oxygen, hydrogen, electrons and
oxidation number. Assign oxidation numbers using the standard rules. Identify the
oxidising and the reducing agent in a given reaction. Write and balance
half-equations and combine them into an overall ionic equation. Name common
oxidising and reducing agents and the tests for them.

## Four ways of saying the same thing

Oxidation is the addition of oxygen, the removal of hydrogen, the loss of
electrons, or an increase in oxidation number. Reduction is the reverse of each.
The memory aid is OIL RIG: oxidation is loss, reduction is gain, of electrons.

The two always happen together, which is why such a reaction is called a redox
reaction. The substance that is reduced is the oxidising agent, because it did
the oxidising; the substance that is oxidised is the reducing agent.

In

CuO + H2 -> Cu + H2O

the copper(II) oxide loses oxygen, so it is reduced, and it is the oxidising
agent. The hydrogen gains oxygen, so it is oxidised, and it is the reducing
agent.

## Rules for oxidation numbers

1. The oxidation number of an element in its free state is zero: Na, O2, Cl2, S8
   are all zero.
2. The oxidation number of a simple ion equals its charge: +1 for Na+, -2 for
   O2-, +3 for Al3+.
3. In a compound, hydrogen is +1, except in metallic hydrides such as NaH where
   it is -1.
4. In a compound, oxygen is -2, except in peroxides such as H2O2 where it is -1,
   and in OF2 where it is +2.
5. Group I metals are +1 and Group II metals are +2 in all their compounds.
   Fluorine is always -1.
6. The oxidation numbers in a neutral compound add to zero; in a polyatomic ion
   they add to the charge on the ion.

Worked figures. In H2SO4, let the oxidation number of sulphur be x. Then
2(+1) + x + 4(-2) = 0, so 2 + x - 8 = 0 and x = +6. In the trioxonitrate(V) ion
NO3-, x + 3(-2) = -1, so x - 6 = -1 and x = +5. In KMnO4,
(+1) + x + 4(-2) = 0, so x = +7. In the tetraoxomanganate(VII) reduction to
Mn2+, manganese goes from +7 to +2, a fall of five, so five electrons are gained
per manganese atom.

## Half-equations and the overall ionic equation

A half-equation shows one half of a redox change on its own, with electrons
written in explicitly. To balance one in acid solution: balance the atoms other
than oxygen and hydrogen; add water to balance the oxygen; add hydrogen ions to
balance the hydrogen; then add electrons to balance the charge.

For the tetraoxomanganate(VII) ion in acid:

MnO4- + 8H+ + 5e- -> Mn2+ + 4H2O

Check it. Oxygen: four on the left in MnO4-, four on the right in 4H2O. Hydrogen:
eight on each side. Charge: (-1) + (+8) + (-5) = +2 on the left, and +2 on the
right. It balances.

For iron(II) being oxidised:

Fe2+ -> Fe3+ + e-

To combine two half-equations, multiply each until the electrons cancel. Here the
iron half-equation is multiplied by five:

MnO4- + 5Fe2+ + 8H+ -> Mn2+ + 5Fe3+ + 4H2O

## Common oxidising and reducing agents, and their tests

Oxidising agents: acidified KMnO4, which turns from purple to colourless;
acidified K2Cr2O7, which turns from orange to green; chlorine; concentrated
H2SO4 and concentrated HNO3; oxygen; hydrogen peroxide.

Reducing agents: hydrogen; carbon and carbon(II) oxide; hydrogen sulphide, which
deposits yellow sulphur; sulphur(IV) oxide and sulphites; iron(II) salts;
reactive metals such as zinc.

A few substances can act either way depending on what they meet. Hydrogen
peroxide oxidises iodide to iodine but reduces acidified KMnO4, releasing oxygen.
Sulphur(IV) oxide usually reduces, but it oxidises hydrogen sulphide to sulphur.

## Not on this syllabus

Standard electrode potentials and the calculation of cell e.m.f. from them, the
Nernst equation, the electrochemical series used quantitatively to predict
feasibility, disproportionation treated as a formal named category with
quantitative work, and redox titration calculations with their own indicators are
not examined here.
