## What the syllabus requires

Explain why atoms combine. Describe ionic, covalent, co-ordinate (dative),
metallic and hydrogen bonding, and van der Waals forces, and give an example of
each. Relate the type of bonding in a substance to its melting point,
solubility and electrical conductivity. Work out the formula of an ionic compound
from the charges on its ions.

## Why atoms combine

Atoms combine to reach the stable electron arrangement of a noble gas, which for
most of the elements in this syllabus means eight electrons in the outer shell,
or two in the case of hydrogen, lithium and beryllium. They do this either by
transferring electrons, which gives ionic bonding, or by sharing them, which
gives covalent bonding.

## Ionic bonding

An ionic bond is the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions
formed by the complete transfer of electrons from a metal atom to a non-metal
atom.

Sodium (2, 8, 1) loses its single outer electron to become Na+ with the
configuration 2, 8. Chlorine (2, 8, 7) gains one electron to become Cl- with the
configuration 2, 8, 8. One atom of each is needed, so the formula is NaCl.

Magnesium (2, 8, 2) loses two electrons to become Mg2+. Each chlorine atom can
accept only one, so two are needed and the formula is MgCl2. The rule is that the
total positive charge must equal the total negative charge: for Al3+ and O2-, the
lowest whole numbers that balance are 2 and 3, giving Al2O3.

Ionic compounds form giant ionic lattices. They have high melting and boiling
points because a great deal of energy is needed to overcome the attraction
between ions throughout the lattice; they are usually soluble in water and
insoluble in organic solvents; and they conduct electricity only when molten or
dissolved, because only then are the ions free to move.

## Covalent, co-ordinate, metallic and weaker forces

A covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons, one from each atom, and it forms
between non-metal atoms. Hydrogen shares one pair in H2, oxygen shares two pairs
in O2 (a double bond), and nitrogen shares three pairs in N2 (a triple bond).
Methane, CH4, has four single covalent bonds.

Simple covalent substances form small molecules held to each other by weak
forces, so they have low melting and boiling points and do not conduct
electricity. Giant covalent substances are the exception: in diamond every carbon
atom is bonded to four others throughout the crystal, giving an extremely high
melting point and great hardness, while graphite has layers of carbon atoms each
bonded to three others, leaving one electron per atom free to move, which is why
graphite conducts electricity and diamond does not.

A co-ordinate or dative bond is a covalent bond in which both shared electrons
come from the same atom. It forms when ammonia's lone pair is donated to a
hydrogen ion to give the ammonium ion, NH4+, and when ammonia or water donates a
lone pair to a metal ion. Once formed, a co-ordinate bond is identical to an
ordinary covalent bond.

Metallic bonding is the attraction between a lattice of positive metal ions and
the sea of delocalised electrons that the atoms have released. It explains why
metals conduct heat and electricity, and why they are malleable and ductile: the
layers of ions slide over one another without breaking the bonding.

Hydrogen bonding is the attraction between a hydrogen atom bonded to a small
highly electronegative atom, that is nitrogen, oxygen or fluorine, and a lone
pair on another such atom. It is far weaker than a covalent bond but much
stronger than an ordinary intermolecular force, and it is why water is a liquid
at room temperature while hydrogen sulphide, a heavier molecule, is a gas, why
ice is less dense than water, and why ethanol and the lower alkanoic acids mix
freely with water.

Van der Waals forces are the weak attractions between all molecules, arising from
momentary uneven distributions of electrons. They increase with molecular size,
which is why the boiling points of the alkanes and of the halogens rise down
their series.

## Not on this syllabus

Molecular-orbital theory, hybridisation of atomic orbitals, VSEPR as a formal
predictive theory of bond angles, lattice-energy and Born-Haber cycle
calculations, crystal-field and ligand-field theory of transition-metal complex
ions, and dipole-moment calculations are not examined here.
